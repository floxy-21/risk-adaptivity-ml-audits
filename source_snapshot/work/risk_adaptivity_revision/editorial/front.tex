\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background.} When verification records are incomplete, an auditor
needs to know whether further readings will make a model comparison precise
enough. We ask when choosing the next reading after seeing an earlier answer
improves that guarantee under a fixed budget.

\textbf{Methods.} We bound model-loss differences over all records compatible
with the available measurements. We classify scalar width penalties and
derive shared-query policies with matching lower bounds and executable
certificates.

\textbf{Results.} A shared-query construction has optimal adaptive mean
joint width $1/2$, versus $2/3$ for randomized batching, with matching
lower bounds. For scalar width, the two minimax risks agree universally
exactly for nondecreasing concave penalties. On 165 of 819 original blocks and 57 of 279 external
blocks, two shared adaptive readings guarantee joint half-width precision,
whereas optimal randomized-batch worst-case success is exactly 50\%.
On these subsets, recorded success is 100\% versus 65.45\% and 64.91\%
for certified optimal batch mixtures. Across the complete panels,
adaptive gains over fixed worst-width batches are 7.81 and 11.11 percentage
points; gains over greedy adaptation are 2.20 and 2.15 points. Under
illustrative 1\% internal errors with exact endpoints, 45 external blocks
retain an adaptive guarantee that every randomized batch fails to match.
Capacity information reduces this advantage, and measured public-API retrieval
favors bulk.

\textbf{Conclusions.} These guarantees depend on the supplied bounds and
access rules. The shared-budget evidence is retrospective; physical meter
accuracy and improved downstream model choice are not established.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} artificial intelligence; machine learning;
adaptive information; minimax risk; optimal recovery; robust optimization.

\section{Introduction}
A model audit may need additional measurements before it can justify a
comparison. The auditor can retrieve the full record, choose a batch of
readings in advance, or choose later readings after seeing earlier answers.
We call the last option adaptive querying. A sharp interval is the smallest
interval containing the loss difference for every record compatible with
the available information. We ask when feedback improves the
guarantee that this interval will be sufficiently narrow. Width limits how
much the reported loss difference can vary across compatible records.
The application requires retrieval to halve that range: a declared uncertainty
budget for reporting a comparison, not a validated proxy for choosing a winner.
A narrow interval can still contain zero.

We study affine scalar contrasts on nonempty compact convex uncertainty
sets, with queries selected from a fixed finite dictionary of linear
measurements. This formulation includes paired squared-loss comparisons,
overlapping linear constraints, valid coordinate bounds, signed targets,
and fixed query costs. Batch and adaptive policies have the same hard
budget. The unknown record is fixed before the algorithm's random seed is
drawn; probabilities describe acquisition randomization, not a distribution
assumed for the missing truth. Fixed bounded measurement errors can be
included by enlarging the hidden record when the resulting uncertainty set
is compact and convex.

The practical decision is which retrieval protocol can meet the auditor's
required precision. We use fixed model predictions and choose which
verification readings to retrieve. A certificate checks whether the resulting
comparison meets the requirement for every compatible record. This differs
from improving the predictor itself.

The central result is a shared-query separation with explicit policies and
matching lower bounds (Section~\ref{sec:joint-separations}). A six-interval
construction has optimal mean joint width $1/2$ for adaptive policies and
$2/3$ for randomized batches. A worked meter example then certifies a
different objective: feedback guarantees precision that no randomized
fixed batch can guarantee. Both arguments have executable proof certificates.
These results build on the scalar classification: concavity exactly
characterizes the nondecreasing width penalties for which adaptation never
improves randomized minimax risk. A coordinate-query construction proves
the converse. The classification credits established minimax reasoning and
Jensen's inequality; a dimension-dependent precision consequence adapts
established sparse-policy bounds \citep{kurtz2026}.

Cumulative meter records let us ask a concrete question: can the same
two readings make all six comparisons precise at once? On all 819 original
and 279 external blocks, the shared adaptive policy improves recorded joint
precision success by 7.81 and 11.11 percentage points over the best fixed
worst-width batch. The gains over a strong greedy adaptive policy are 2.20
and 2.15 points. This is a retrospective extension using the frozen data,
not an additional independent sample. Bounded-error certificates and capacity
controls test when the shared-query advantage persists.
An objective-matched calculation identifies 165 original and 57 external
blocks where adaptive success is guaranteed and the full-continuum
randomized-batch precision optimum is exactly $1/2$. On those same blocks,
the recorded batch success is 65.45\% and 64.91\%, versus 100\% for adaptive
querying. The subset criterion uses the forecast geometry, not these recorded
outcomes; the complete panels and their stronger empirical comparators remain
in the evaluation.

The retrieval service determines which choices are useful. We test bounded
reading errors and hourly capacity information, which can respectively
increase the required budget or remove the need for feedback. Bulk retrieval
is preferable on the measured public API. Our implementation therefore checks
the permitted measurements and resource limits before choosing among supplied
bulk, fixed and adaptive certificates.

The paper develops the classification and sharp examples, then computable
two-query guarantees and their cumulative-meter specialization. The
evaluation reports fixed protocols, recorded replay, uncertainty sensitivity
and actual retrieval measurements. Online Resource~1 provides extended
proofs, the separate compact coordinate-query solver, historical controls,
complete case ledgers and executable verification.

\subsection{Related work and the contribution boundary}
Partial identification studies conclusions that survive incomplete
observations \citep{manski,polo}. For model comparison, cancellation in
paired squared losses leaves an affine function of the unknown truth;
this is established loss structure rather than a new loss function
\citep{patton}. Active evaluation and model selection acquire labels for
estimating performance or choosing among predictors
\citep{sawade,hara,active,okanovic2025}. Our target is the entire sharp
interval of a fixed-record contrast after exact observations. No estimator
of the population risk, surrogate label distribution, or new forecasting
architecture is introduced.

The width of an information fiber is an established local diameter of
information in optimal recovery \citep{novak2008}. Adaptivity and
randomization for approximation of linear operators on convex classes
have a substantial literature \citep{osipenko,krieg2025,krieg2026}.
The objective here must be distinguished both from actual reconstruction
error and from an expected global radius. For a scalar fiber, its radius
is half its width, but the risk considered here is
$\sup_y\mathbb E_\omega W_{Q_\omega(y)}(y)$: first average the local
width at a fixed record, then take the worst record. In general this is
different from averaging a worst-fiber radius after each random choice
of information. Nor is actual point-estimation error at $y$ necessarily
half the width of its information fiber. The theorem does not deny
adaptivity benefits for those other objectives.

Our minimax interchange is an application of \citet{sion1958}, and its
concave-penalty upper bound is Jensen's inequality. The claim is the
resulting universal penalty classification, its coordinate-query
converse, and the exact precision and moment separations. We make no
claim of inventing minimax theory, local diameter, or the general concept
of adaptive information. The bounded literature comparison identifies
these precise objectives and assumptions; it is not a proof of historical
priority over every optimal-recovery result.

Information-dependent decisions are also established in adjustable robust
optimization \citep{adjust2024,ddid}. The dimension-dependent precision
bound below adapts the sufficient-policy result of
\citet[Theorem~2 and Example~5]{kurtz2026}; its query interpretation and
sharp examples are made explicit. Weighted-oracle randomized optimization
and dynamic-program LP constructions are established
\citep{kawase2024,martin1990}. The supporting aggregate solver contributes
a structured hard-budget batch representation and dual-completion
certificate (Online Resource~1, S5/S7). Secondary optimization among tied
robust solutions follows an established concern \citep{iancu2014}.

Adaptive sampling of cumulative information has close precedents.
\citet[Section~4.1.1, Corollary~1 and Theorem~3]{gaillard2023}
study certified scalar integral estimation from sequential evaluations of
a monotone function. For monotone contrast coefficients, summation by parts
relates the register problem to weighted monotone integration. Our instance
test pairs an adaptive precision guarantee with an adverse distribution
limiting every randomized batch under the same information interface.

\citet[Theorem~1 and Proposition~1]{kuboyama2026} gives exact model-choice
certificates after adaptive binary labels and compares shared-label paired
bounds with separate intervals. That work also distinguishes hindsight
certificate size from acquisition requirements (Theorem~2 and Proposition~3).
Our application uses constrained continuous targets, while the central
classification concerns the penalty of terminal sharp width. These precise
distinctions, rather than certification or shared comparisons generally,
define the contribution relative to model-choice and information-discovery
frameworks. The shared-query objective is the established weighted
$\ell_\infty$ local diameter of a vector target, not a new uncertainty measure.
Our additional result is its exact two-prefix separation and computable
guarantees for joint forecast comparisons. Neither shared observations nor
adaptive vector recovery alone is claimed as new.

\section{Model Comparisons and Sharp Information}
Let $a,b\in\mathbb R^N$ be two fixed prediction vectors for the same
scheduled targets. Their paired mean-squared-error difference is
\begin{equation}
 D(y)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\{(a_i-y_i)^2-(b_i-y_i)^2\}
 =d+c^\top y,\qquad c_i=\frac{2(b_i-a_i)}N.
 \label{eq:linear}
\end{equation}
All scheduled targets remain in the denominator. Initially observed values,
documented aggregate measurements and other defensible constraints define
the feasible set $F$. Any permitted archive record that directly reconstructs
a target is incorporated before this set is declared. Sharpness is always
relative to that declared information set.

The attainable interval of $D$ on $F$ is an identification interval for
this record, not a sampling confidence interval. A negative upper endpoint
certifies that $a$ has smaller squared loss for every completion; a positive
lower endpoint certifies $b$. An interval containing zero cannot certify
a strict winner. Subsequent exact measurements restrict $F$ to a fiber and
therefore cannot widen its sharp interval. A prescribed width threshold
instead asks for a precision guarantee, regardless of the interval's location.

The mathematical examples below also describe paired squared-loss widths.
Given any contrast coefficient $c$, nonnegative predictions
$a_i=N\max(-c_i,0)/2$ and $b_i=N\max(c_i,0)/2$ realize that coefficient
in \eqref{eq:linear}. The induced constant changes the interval's position,
but not its width. Thus the constructions have a model-comparison
interpretation without being measurements of prediction performance.
