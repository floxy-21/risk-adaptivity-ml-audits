\section{S2. Historical certification, gates and controls}
\subsection{Archive-consistent certification of saved ML forecasts}
\label{sec:historical-application}
The historical study uses 30 Portugal consumption series, one French
household series and three recorded cumulative-counter series. The four
forecasters are persistence, ridge regression, histogram gradient boosting
and a multilayer perceptron. Forecasts, chronological training periods,
eligibility filters and masks are reused unchanged. Appendix~\ref{app:historical-design}
specifies the protocol, training history and information boundary.

The archive-aware evaluator receives all saved test forecast rows within
the declared period. In particular, a saved persistence forecast
$p_{t+24}^{\rm pers}=y_t$ reveals its earlier target directly. These copies
and exact aggregate deductions are applied before all methods, using
source-row traces; hidden reference targets only check the recovered
values. No inverse reconstruction from other fitted predictor mappings is
assumed. Later records within the evaluation period are included. The
whole-period feasible set is the product of the remaining daily blocks
after this closure, and equal-length daily endpoints may therefore be
averaged. This is a retrospective benchmark archive rule, not a measured
production arrival deadline.

The complete Portugal archive-aware exact-total grid contains 9,720
repeated model-pair comparisons across gap lengths, mechanisms, seeds and
aggregate resolutions. Direct paired bounds certify 7,836 (80.62\%),
whereas independently bounded individual losses certify 2,513 (25.85\%).
The additional 5,323 certificates are a 54.76-percentage-point difference
under identical information and zero additional queries. In the six-hour-gap
illustrative cell the counts are 478 of 540 versus 64 of 540. The broader
grid, rather than that cell, supplies the abstract's empirical result.

Independent subtraction of sharp individual-loss bounds encloses the
paired interval but may combine extremizers that cannot be the same
record. For example, predictions $(1,1)$ and $(2,2)$ under
$y_1+y_2=2$, $y\geq0$, have individual MSE ranges $[0,1]$ and $[1,2]$;
separate subtraction gives $[-2,0]$, whereas the paired contrast is exactly
$-1$. For exact totals, each individual-loss minimum is a simplex
projection and each maximum is attained at a simplex vertex. For uncertain
totals, the remaining block instead satisfies
$x\geq0$ and $L\leq\sum_i x_i\leq U$, after subtracting observed contributions.
The minimum is the projection of its prediction vector onto this band.
If the nonnegative part of that vector has a sum in $[L,U]$, it is already
optimal and the minimizing total may be interior. Otherwise the projection
uses the lower or upper boundary simplex, respectively. The maximum of
the convex individual squared loss is attained at a vertex of the compact
band polytope.

The complete 34-series scenario grid contains 66,096 repeated comparisons:
10,404 are certified by both methods, 23,906 only by pairing and 31,786
by neither. None is certified only by separate-loss bounds. All 324
scenario cells, including unresolved cases, appear in the supplement;
Appendix~\ref{app:certificate-grid} displays their 18 cohort/access/tolerance
aggregates. The checker verifies the saved sign
rule, paired-interval nesting and reference enclosure. The redistributable
endpoint replay covers 60,264 Portugal/household comparisons; the 5,832
counter comparisons use local original endpoints and require the documented
raw-source regeneration for an external replay. These are different
reproduction levels, not interchangeable counts of independent evidence.

The audit adds all-completions support to a point evaluation. The historical
donor makes zero observed
whole-period errors at full coverage on the 9,720 Portugal, 324 household
and 972 counter exact-total comparisons. Those empirical successes do not
give it an all-completions guarantee. Our certificate can accompany a point
evaluation by identifying which rankings its declared information actually
forces; this study does not measure consequential downstream decisions
changed by that certificate.

\subsection{A certificate gate for proposed model comparisons}
\label{sec:practical_gate}
An imputation rule can propose a model ranking before its robustness to
missing verification targets has been established. We implement a separate
gate that receives that proposed direction and the two certificate endpoints,
and returns \emph{allow}, \emph{hold}, or \emph{contradict}. A proposal is allowed
only when the entire interval supports its direction, using the existing
scaled $10^{-9}$ sign margin. The gate accepts no hidden reference targets.
Its output supports retrospective model-comparison decisions.

On the original Portugal archive-aware exact-total grid, paired certificates
allow 7,836 of 9,720 historical-donor proposals, compared with 2,513 for
separate-loss certificates under identical information and zero additional
queries. The number held falls from 7,207 to 1,884, a derived reduction of
73.86\% in unresolved certificates. All 1,884 held paired comparisons have a
sharp endpoint supporting the opposite ranking beyond the numerical decision
margin. Consequently, the declared information admits an opposing completion
even though the donor's realized ranking is correct in every case. Neither
certificate method contradicts a donor proposal in this grid. This result
quantifies the distinction between an observed correct imputation and an
all-completions certificate. Separate-loss intervals are the matched
certificate comparator, not the strongest point-imputation predictor.

The executable action ledger covers all 20,088 public exact-total comparisons
across both access regimes and both public cohorts. It fixes gate decisions
before consulting reference contrasts. Constructing 40,176 gate outputs and
their ledger columns took 0.462\,s after CSV parsing; the complete replay took
7.704\,s. These are postprocessing times for frozen endpoints, not times for
regenerating the forecast archive or computing all bounds.

\subsection{Authenticating a selected model against all alternatives}
\label{sec:practical-model-selection}
A practical certificate gate can accompany an existing model-selection
rule. First, the historical donor imputation proposes the model with the
smallest completed-period MSE among persistence, ridge, gradient boosting
and the MLP. Second, the gate authenticates that proposal only if its
paired interval certifies a lower MSE than each of the other three models.
Otherwise it returns unresolved. Selection uses the saved donor loss
differences alone; hidden targets are inspected only after selection and
certification. A unique donor preference is required, and nonunique cases
would remain in the denominator. Both bound methods receive the same
proposed model and the same archive information, with zero additional
queries.

This secondary analysis aggregates the existing six pair comparisons into
one four-model selection scenario; it is not an independent replication.
For the Portugal exact-total archive, paired bounds authenticate 933 of
1,620 proposals (57.59\%), compared with 167 (10.31\%) using separate-loss
bounds. The difference is 47.28 percentage points, with a descriptive
whole-consumer bootstrap interval of 40.80--53.77 points. Unresolved
proposals decrease from 1,453 to 687, a 52.72\% reduction relative to the
separate-bound unresolved count. This is a reduction in unresolved
benchmark cases, not measured staff time, retrieval cost or deployment
failures. The proposed models are gradient boosting in 1,242 scenarios,
the MLP in 216 and ridge in 162; these repeated scenarios share 30 consumers.

\subsection{Executable audit decisions and independent reconstruction}
\label{sec:executable-gate}
The supplement includes a working audit interface, beyond a table of saved
endpoints. It accepts surviving readings, fixed predictions, declared
totals and permitted persistence-copy records. It reconstructs targets
from the archive, computes all six paired intervals, and returns attaining
endpoint completions. A ranking is allowed only when its entire interval
has the required sign; otherwise the interface withholds the certificate.
A best model is reported only if it is certified against every alternative.
The input schema rejects a hidden-reference-target field. Source-row traces
and residual-total deductions accompany the output.

Two actual frozen-forecast examples exercise this interface. Selection
uses the first six-hour contiguous-mask record with permitted copies and
the first without in the existing source ordering, rather than favorable
model errors. For consumer MT\_001 on 1 January 2014, all six missing
readings are reconstructed and all six pair rankings are certified; on
28 January, six readings remain unknown, one comparison is certified and
five are withheld. The latter persistence-minus-ridge MSE interval is
$[0.85544,2.93641]$, certifying ridge for that comparison but not a best
model overall. Both examples use zero paid readings. Held reference targets
enter the subsequent checker only. The totals and missingness remain
constructed benchmark inputs, not newly observed production failures.

Twenty-four independently assembled endpoint LPs verify these outputs.
The maximum absolute endpoint disagreement is $1.78\times10^{-15}$,
with zero recorded known-coordinate and original-total witness residuals.
The acceptance threshold is $2\times10^{-8}$ times the maximum of one,
the absolute interval endpoints and the aggregate totals; HiGHS feasibility
tolerances are $10^{-9}$. Small residual arithmetic corrections are recorded.
This is floating-point verification,
not formal interval arithmetic. These examples demonstrate usable
decision and witness outputs, not an additional performance estimate.

A second checker independently reconstructs two fixed scenarios for all
30 additional consumers: 6,420 daily snapshots and 360 period comparisons.
It uses 1,800 calls to a separately written reference bound implementation
and 1,200 water-filling/vertex individual-loss optimizations, without
importing production closure or bound routines. Maximum normalized
endpoint differences are $1.07\times10^{-14}$ for paired bounds and
$1.10\times10^{-14}$ for separate-loss bounds, within a $2\times10^{-8}$
checking tolerance. It also checks all 19,440 ledger counts and issued
sign decisions. Together with the whole-period LP checks, this validates
both sides of the new comparison rather than its summary arithmetic alone.

\subsection{The retained adverse recovery result}
On the original Portugal six-hour-gap archive, saved persistence copies
reconstruct 95.33\% of masked target occurrences for free. Daily certification
already reaches 96.67\% before paid recovery and 98.71\% after at most two
readings per model pair; the exact-over-greedy addition is 0.042 percentage
points. A budget for one pair is not a shared budget for all six pairs.
No monetary retrieval cost or field latency was measured.

In the archived randomized comparison, all policies see the same closed
archive. On Portugal, normalized worst width decreases from 29.595\% to
26.673\% of initial width, but expected width at the actual record increases
from 20.154\% to 23.670\% (17.45\% relative). Certification decreases
from 98.795\% to 98.688\%. These are exact expectations over the computed
mixtures, not Monte Carlo estimates. Width fractions first sum within
each meter and then average meter ratios. The two-reading budget applies
separately to each day and pair, including already certified comparisons.

Across 1,074 positive-width public cases, expected realized width improves
in 14, worsens in 649 and ties in 411. Both target-independent refinements
in Section~\ref{sec:tie-selection} completed on all 1,074 cases without
removing the penalty beyond the declared tolerance. This does not imply
every optimal mixture has identical realized performance. The new theorem
establishes that adaptation cannot improve the robust mean-width value;
it does not turn these adverse observed outcomes into an operational benefit.
The compact solver is therefore an exact risk optimizer, not an empirically
dominant default acquisition rule.

\section{S3. Full historical design and reproducibility}\label{app:historical-design}
The following specifications document the reused historical study. Its
archived broader recovery tests are distinguished from this revision's
new mathematical checks and extended additional-consumer grid.
No new forecast fit is introduced.

\subsection{Data sources and chronological splits}
The primary evaluation uses 34 consumption series from three public sources
(Table~\ref{tab:data}). The Portugal panel uses the first 30 eligible meter
IDs in the ordered UCI ElectricityLoadDiagrams20112014 file \citep{portugal}.
Eligibility requires at least 99\% finite and more than 95\% positive
training-hour values. The training year is 2013; test windows are the first
28 days of January, April, July, and October 2014. The original end-stamped
15-minute average-kW readings are shifted to interval starts and converted
to hourly energy by summing four values and dividing by four.

The UCI French household trace \citep{uci} supplies a separate scale and
load pattern. Training uses 2008--2009 and testing uses eligible days in
2010. Hourly kWh is the sum of 60 minute-average kW values divided by 60;
all 60 readings must be valid. For both UCI sources, the spring and autumn
clock-transition dates and target days whose 1-, 2-, or 7-day lags touch
them are excluded in training and testing. This prevents documented local
time-grid artifacts from contaminating the features. Every eligible day
must contain all 24 targets and all required causal features. The retained
totals in these two sources are constructed from the complete trace before
masking; they are not separate backup-register measurements.

The third source contains actual cumulative Discovergy consumption counters
released with the BLEM study \citep{blem}. We use the first three consumer
IDs in the pinned source version identified in the supplement. Each raw
series contains 175,201 readings on an uninterrupted three-minute grid,
without counter decreases. Hourly energy is calculated from integer
counter differences at exact UTC hour endpoints, using the source's
$10^{-10}$ conversion to kWh. Training uses January--September 2017 and
testing uses October--December 2017, subject to complete-day and feature
availability. First and last partial days are excluded. Here the retained
six-hour or daily totals can be obtained directly from the recorded
counter endpoints. Removing interval targets is still an artificial outage:
the experiment assumes these endpoints remain available and does not
measure an independent communication channel's survival rate.

\subsection{Forecasts and information cutoffs}
The four candidates are previous-day persistence, ridge regression,
histogram gradient boosting, and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) neural
network. Boosting and the MLP provide nonlinear AI models; persistence and
ridge provide simple references. Each forecast supplies all 24 targets at
the start of a day. In this retrospective benchmark, issue time is a
simulated historical day-start cutoff enforced through feature timestamps;
these are not contemporaneously logged vendor forecasts.
Features for target hour $h$ are lagged values at 24, 48, and 168 hours,
the mean of the previous day, and sine/cosine encodings of hour, weekday,
and month. All features are available at the common issue time. The
regression models share parameters across the 24 target hours.

Ridge uses training-only standardization and regularization parameter 1.
Gradient boosting uses 100 iterations, learning rate 0.08, at most 15
leaves, minimum leaf size 20, $L_2$ regularization 1, 127 bins, and seed
1729. The MLP has two hidden layers of 32 and 16 rectified-linear units,
Adam optimization, learning rate 0.001, $L_2$ penalty 0.001, minibatches
of 256, and at most 1000 epochs, with seed 1729. Its features are standardized
using training data only; targets are divided by the training mean and
predictions are rescaled to kWh. Validation-based early stopping is disabled;
the library's training-loss convergence rule remains enabled. The supplement
records iterations, final training losses, fit times, and warnings for every
meter. Negative predictions are clipped to zero. The configurations are
fixed before the final evaluation run; no search on its evaluation outcomes is
performed. Training periods,
data-quality filters, and the experiment protocol are stored with the
reproduction code. Forecasts are generated once and reused for every
verification scenario. The experiment isolates missing \emph{verification}
targets, not damaged inputs at forecast issue time.

\paragraph{Fresh model-fitting verification.}
As archived verification of the earlier study, a fresh fitting replay was executed for all 34 original series plus a separate 30-consumer Portugal replication cohort from the same source/calendar, from the pinned raw sources, bypassing the previous hourly caches and any fitted model state. All 192 Ridge, histogram-boosting, and MLP estimators completed under the declared settings and seeds; daily persistence was reconstructed. No training warnings were captured, and MLP stopping epochs ranged from 28 to 443. A separately written checker found exact numerical agreement after CSV parsing for all 166,536 targets and 666,144 forecast values, checked 768 complete-reference MSE/RMSE/MAE quantities and 384 model-pair rankings with no ranking changes, and independently reconstructed the feature and train/test eligibility calculations for all 64 series. On the same Intel Core i7-8565U host, raw-data preparation took 52.8~s and the sequential fresh fitting phase took 262.9~s with two numerical threads; these are descriptive shared-host measurements. This verifies fresh execution of the original trainer with independent arithmetic and chronology checks. It does not constitute an independent training implementation, a new cohort, or another run of the downstream mask grids.

\subsection{Missingness and retained totals}
For each eligible 24-target record we remove exactly 2, 6, or 12 targets.
The mechanisms are a contiguous gap with a random start, a random scattered
set, and the highest-demand targets. The last is an oracle stress test of
selective missingness, not an estimated mechanism of real communication
failure. Disjoint 6-hour and 24-hour totals are retained. These alternatives
use the same masks within each mechanism, record, and seed. Three seeds
are used for stochastic masks. The deterministic high-demand condition is
not treated as additional independent evidence when repeated in the design.

The exact-total setting is augmented by symmetric uncertainty intervals of
1\% and 5\% of each complete aggregate. Bounds use the widened feasible
set; point-imputation comparators continue to use the central supplied
total. This isolates sensitivity to uncertainty in the surviving totals.
All methods see the same surviving interval values. No learned load cap,
smoothness constraint, or hidden high-resolution target is supplied to an
imputer or bound calculation.

\subsection{Comparators and decisions}
The direct paired bounds are compared with sharp individual MSE bounds
combined by independent subtraction. Both methods can decline to establish
a strict ordering. The point-evaluation comparators are complete-case
scoring, uniform allocation of missing energy, allocation in proportion to
a weekday/hour profile estimated from training data, and an energy-rescaled
historical donor selected using surviving target coordinates. The donor
comparator is inspired by the energy-preservation principle of CPI
\citep{cpi}; it is not presented as a reproduction of CPI's complete
published algorithm. Training-based shapes are independent of the competing
test forecasts. If a shape has zero mass on a missing block, uniform
allocation supplies the fallback.

Each energy-preserving filling subtracts the observed consumption from
the retained total, then distributes that residual over missing intervals.
The complete reference is used only for evaluation of the resulting
ordering. Complete-case scoring uses its common observed-count
denominator; only its ordering is compared with the full-record target.
Its numerical loss magnitude is not substituted for a full-day MSE.

For each pair, a decision is correct if its strict sign agrees with the
complete-reference MSE difference. We report the proportion of comparisons
resolved and errors among those decisions, together with the rate of
unresolved comparisons. A method can achieve zero decision errors by
always declining, so resolution is essential. 

\subsection{Archive-consistent whole-period audits}\label{sec:period-information}
The historical audit covers all eligible test days of one meter and one
fixed model pair. Its target is the paired MSE over those same hourly
coordinates, with denominator $N=24d$ for $d$ retained days. The benchmark
snapshot specifies surviving hourly readings, six-hour or daily aggregate
bands, and the permitted saved forecast records. Actual production arrival
times are unavailable; this is a retrospective access rule, not a measured
deadline or latency experiment.

The operative \emph{saved-forecast archive} regime supplies every saved
test forecast row for that meter and all four candidates. For each masked
target at time $t$, an available daily-persistence prediction at $t+24$
provides $y_t$ directly. This includes later forecast rows inside the
evaluation period. The available saved test archive and the within-period
raw forecast vectors coincide in this benchmark; the result uses no
additional outside-period forecasts. Reconstruction reads timestamps and that forecast
column only; hidden reference targets subsequently check the copied value.
Every copy has a source-row trace. Training records used by the profile
and donor baselines remain confined to the original chronological training
period. Unsaved forecasts and feature caches are excluded. The feasible set
does not impose inverse constraints from other fitted predictor mappings,
including the ridge model; all four prediction columns are used for scoring,
but only the documented direct persistence identity is used for reconstruction.

An \emph{isolated-daily summary} reference supplies each daily evaluator
only that day's forecasts, surviving readings and aggregates. The period
aggregator receives scalar comparison endpoints, point-score sums and
counts, not cross-day raw forecast vectors. This deliberately limited
reference is not the information set of an analyst holding the complete
period's raw predictions. Both regimes also deduce a remaining singleton
from an exact residual total and all remaining zeros from a zero upper
residual total. A singleton with a positive-width total interval remains
unknown. The original daily experiments retain their frozen supplied-input
protocol; they are not relabelled as reanalyses with these enlarged observations.

Writing $O_0$ for surviving readings, $R(\mathcal A)$ for verified direct
copies from the permitted archive and $C$ for aggregate-determined values,
the new observed set is $O_{\mathcal A}=O_0\cup R(\mathcal A)\cup C$.
For exact totals the comparison set is
\begin{equation}
 \F_{\mathcal A}=\{y:y_i=o_i\ (i\in O_{\mathcal A}),\quad
 y_i\geq0\ (i\notin O_{\mathcal A}),\quad
 \textstyle\sum_{i\in I_g}y_i=T_g\ \text{for every }g\}.
 \label{eq:archive-set}
\end{equation}
For interval totals, replace the last equality by its declared lower and
upper bounds. Once copies fix coordinates, the remaining blocks are still

\section{S5. Full structured coordinate-recovery construction}
\section{Compact Recovery with Disjoint Aggregate Totals}
\label{sec:structured}
The general theory applies to any compact convex $F$, but efficient
optimization still depends on its geometry. Consider residual targets
$y_{gi}\geq0$ in disjoint blocks $\mathcal M_g$, with exact sums
$\sum_i y_{gi}=E_g$. Here the intercept $d$ includes all fixed observed
contributions to the contrast; only unknown coordinates remain in the
residual notation. Write
$\F=\prod_g\{y_g\geq0:\sum_i y_{gi}=E_g\}$ for this residual
representation of the full feasible record $F$. The sharp paired interval has endpoints
\begin{equation}
 D^-=d+\sum_g E_g\min_{i\in\mathcal M_g}c_{gi},\qquad
 D^+=d+\sum_g E_g\max_{i\in\mathcal M_g}c_{gi}.
 \label{eq:sharp}
\end{equation}
Blocks with no missing coordinates are omitted after checking that their
observed sums equal the recorded totals.
Each extremum assigns the block's mass to an extremal coefficient;
disjointness permits simultaneous attainment. A singleton is determined
by its total, and a zero-total block is entirely determined. Negative
residual totals indicate inconsistent input rather than a valid feasible
set. A recorded counter increment must refer to the same units and
interval as the targets; an approximate physical total requires an
appropriately widened constraint.

For queried coordinates $Q_g$ and retained set $S_g=\mathcal M_g\setminus Q_g$,
\begin{equation}
 W(Q,y)=\sum_g\left(\sum_{i\in S_g}y_{gi}\right)
                  \operatorname{range}(c_{g,S_g}).
 \label{eq:querywidth}
\end{equation}
The range of an empty set or singleton is zero. In every block with a
nonempty retained set, all mass can be assigned to unqueried coordinates;
a fully queried block contributes zero width. Thus the worst width of a fixed batch is
$\sum_g E_g\operatorname{range}(c_{g,S_g})$.

\subsection{Deterministic recovery as a reference}
Sort the $m_g$ coefficients in a block. Retaining $m_g-k$ coefficients
with minimum range is achieved by a consecutive window, giving
\begin{equation}
 h_g(k)=E_g\min_{1\leq s\leq k+1}
       (c_{g,(s+m_g-k-1)}-c_{g,(s)}),\quad 0\leq k\leq m_g-1.
 \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
Every retained subset spans a window of that size with no larger range,
which proves the reduction. Allocate queries by
\begin{equation}
 D_B=\min_{\sum_g k_g\leq B}\sum_g h_g(k_g),\qquad
 V(g,b)=\min_k\{V(g-1,b-k)+h_g(k)\}.
 \label{eq:allocation}
\end{equation}
The recurrence minimizes over $0\leq k\leq\min(b,m_g-1)$, has
$V(0,0)=0$ and infeasible states equal to infinity, and extracts
$D_B=\min_{0\leq b\leq B}V(G,b)$ for the at-most budget.
It returns the queried complements of the selected windows. The budget
is a count of distinct acquired coordinates for one contrast, not a
measured monetary cost or a shared budget over all pairs of models.

\begin{proposition}[Deterministic adaptive reference]
\label{prop:det-adapt}
For nonnegative residual targets with disjoint exact totals and unit
costs, the deterministic adaptive worst-width optimum equals $D_B$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Omit queries whose values are already determined and simulate their
answers in the policy's history. Answer zero to every remaining query.
Every positive-total block retains an unqueried coordinate on this
transcript; assign its mass to such a coordinate at termination. This is
a feasible completion realizing the transcript. Its terminal width is
the worst width of the terminal batch and is at least $D_B$. The optimal
fixed batch supplies the reverse inequality.
\end{proof}
This geometry-specific deterministic statement is distinct from the
general randomized mean-width identity. The following program also
solves the randomized adaptive problem for mean width.

\subsection{Randomized recovery under a hard budget}
\label{sec:randomized-recovery}

Randomization permits a different guarantee from deterministic minimax
recovery. We retain exact disjoint totals, nonnegative uncapped targets,
unit query costs, and one fixed forecast contrast. The benchmark first incorporates permitted direct persistence copies
and aggregate-determined values. Sharpness is relative to the declared
feasible set; inverse constraints from other fitted predictors are not
included in the evaluated archive regime.
Remove zero-energy, singleton, and constant-coefficient blocks, whose width
is already zero. Index the remaining blocks by $g=1,\ldots,G$.
If none remain, the optimal width is zero and no query is needed.
Let $\mathcal Q_B$ contain batches with at most $B$ queries in total and
at most $m_g-1$ in block $g$; querying its final coordinate is redundant.
A policy selects $Q\sim\pi$ before reading its answers. The hidden record
$y\in\F$ is fixed before this random draw: the adverse completion may
depend on $\pi$, but cannot depend on the realized batch.

\subsection{Expected width and an exact finite linear program}
Write $S_g(Q)=\M_g\setminus Q$, let $d_g(S)$ denote the coefficient range
on $S$, and set $d_g(S)=0$ for a singleton or empty set. Define
$v_{gi}(Q)=\boldsymbol{1}\{i\in S_g(Q)\}d_g(S_g(Q))$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:querywidth} gives
\begin{equation}
 W(Q,y)=\sum_g\sum_{i\in\M_g}y_{gi} v_{gi}(Q),\qquad
 R_B=\min_{\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal Q_B)}\sup_{y\in\F}
       \mathbb E_{Q\sim\pi}W(Q,y).
 \label{eq:random-objective}
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta(\mathcal Q_B)$ is the set of distributions over admissible
batches. The budget holds for every draw, rather than only in expectation.
The coefficients $c_i$ already include the MSE normalization in
\eqref{eq:linear}.

\begin{proposition}[Finite characterization]
\label{prop:random-finite}
For every batch distribution,
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{y\in\F}\mathbb E_\pi W(Q,y)
 =\sum_g E_g\max_{i\in\M_g}\mathbb E_\pi v_{gi}(Q).
 \label{eq:random-value}
\end{equation}
Consequently $R_B$ is the value of the primal and dual programs
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \min_{\pi,t}\quad &\sum_g E_g t_g,\\
 \text{subject to}\quad
 &\sum_Q\pi_Qv_{gi}(Q)\leq t_g\quad(g,i),\qquad
   \sum_Q\pi_Q=1,\quad\pi_Q\geq0;
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:random-primal}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \max_{w,\eta}\quad &\eta,\\
 \text{subject to}\quad
 &\eta\leq\sum_g\sum_i w_{gi}v_{gi}(Q)\quad(Q\in\mathcal Q_B),\\
 &\sum_i w_{gi}=E_g,\quad w_{gi}\geq0.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:random-dual}
\end{equation}
The variables $t_g$ and $\eta$ are free; the primal inequalities imply
$t_g\geq0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Interchanging finite sums makes the expected width linear in each
block's target vector. Its maximum over a nonnegative simplex of mass
$E_g$ places that mass on a largest expected coefficient. The blocks are
independent, proving \eqref{eq:random-value}. Introducing their maxima
gives the primal. Linear-programming duality gives the stated dual;
the primal is feasible and has a finite optimum.
\end{proof}

Mixed-strategy robust optimization and dual separation are established
methods. Kawase and Sumita provide a general framework based on weighted
scenario optimization \citep{kawase2024}; Mastin, Jaillet, and Chin study
randomized minmax regret and improvements over deterministic strategies
\citep{mastin2015}. The specialization here is the conditional-width
geometry, the exact coordinate-recovery oracle and compact formulation
below, and the adaptive optimality certificate below.

\subsection{An exact pricing oracle}
To separate \eqref{eq:random-dual}, minimize
$\sum_{g,i}w_{gi}v_{gi}(Q)$ over admissible batches. Within a block, order
coefficients increasingly, breaking ties by original coordinate index.
For local budget $k$, put $r=m_g-k$. If $r\geq2$, fix the first and last
retained positions $l<u$, with $u-l+1\geq r$. The least cost for these
endpoints is
\begin{equation}
 (c_{g,(u)}-c_{g,(l)})
 \left(w_{g,(l)}+w_{g,(u)}+
       \sum_{i\in T_{g,l,u,r}}w_{g,(i)}\right),
 \label{eq:random-pricing}
\end{equation}
where $T_{g,l,u,r}$ contains the $r-2$ smallest interior weights.
Every retained subset has such endpoints; once they are fixed, its range
is fixed and these interior choices minimize its nonnegative weighted
cost. Enumerate the endpoints, assign cost zero when $r=1$, and apply
the budget-allocation recurrence across blocks. This supplies exact
polynomial-time separation for rational input. Unlike deterministic
minimum-range recovery, an optimal weighted retained set need not be
a consecutive coefficient window.

\subsection{A compact program and an executable batch distribution}
The following formulation avoids a variable for every possible batch.
Set $q=\min(B,\sum_g(m_g-1))$. Any smaller batch can be padded to $q$
queries without widening its conditional interval for any record, so
requiring exactly $q$ loses no optimality for this objective.
The case $B=0$ is obtained directly from the original width formula.

Construct a directed acyclic graph with nodes $(g,b)$ for completed
blocks $0\leq g\leq G$ and budgets $0\leq b\leq q$. For each admissible
$k$ and endpoint pair $(l,u)$ of a retained set of size $r=m_g-k\geq2$,
create an edge $e=(g,b,k,l,u)$ from $(g-1,b)$ to $(g,b+k)$.
For $r=1$, use a single canonical zero-width edge. Impose nonnegative
unit flow $p_e$ from $(0,0)$ to $(G,q)$, with conservation at all other
nodes. Impossible nodes and edges may be omitted.

For an endpoint edge, introduce interior joint inclusion masses satisfying
\begin{equation}
 0\leq z_{ei}\leq p_e\quad(l<i<u),\qquad
 \sum_{l<i<u}z_{ei}=(r-2)p_e.
 \label{eq:random-hypersimplex}
\end{equation}
Its endpoint inclusion masses are $p_e$; interior inclusion masses are
$z_{ei}$, and all others are zero. Denote these by $\alpha_{ei}$ and
put $d_e=c_{g,(u)}-c_{g,(l)}$. Zero-width edges contribute zero.
The compact program minimizes $\sum_gE_gt_g$ subject to the flow
constraints, \eqref{eq:random-hypersimplex}, and
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{e\text{ for block }g}d_e\alpha_{ei}\leq t_g
 \quad(g,i).
 \label{eq:random-compact}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Exact compact representation]
\label{prop:random-compact}
This program has value $R_B$ and yields an attaining distribution with
at most $B$ queries in every batch. For $B\geq1$, a safe unpruned bound
on its numbers of variables and constraints is
$O(B\sum_g\min(B,m_g)m_g^3)$.
For one block with a fixed local budget, only $O(m_g^3)$ variables are needed.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A distribution over batches induces flows along their budget paths and
the corresponding endpoint and interior inclusion masses, satisfying
all constraints. Conversely, a nonnegative unit flow on an acyclic graph
decomposes into source-to-sink paths. For any edge with $p_e>0$,
the vector $(z_{ei}/p_e)_{l<i<u}$ belongs to
$\{x\in[0,1]^{u-l-1}:\sum_i x_i=r-2\}$. This hypersimplex is the convex
hull of the indicators of its $(r-2)$-element subsets. To see integrality,
any nonintegral feasible vector has at least two fractional entries;
opposite small perturbations of those entries express it as the midpoint
of two distinct feasible vectors, so it is not a vertex.
Thus each positive-flow edge admits a distribution of legal interiors.
Sample a flow path and, conditionally on each selected edge, such an
interior subset. Every resulting batch uses exactly $q$ queries and
has the coordinate coefficients in \eqref{eq:random-compact}. Hence
the two formulations attain the same objective. There are $O(B)$ budget
layers per block, $O(\min(B,m_g))$ local budget choices, $O(m_g^2)$
endpoint choices, and $O(m_g)$ interior variables per endpoint edge,
giving the bound.
\end{proof}

The decomposition is constructive: sample outgoing edges proportionally
to their flows and sample a fixed-cardinality subset with the conditional
interior marginals. For the latter, cumulative marginal intervals and a
uniform offset in $[0,1)$ give systematic sampling with the required
cardinality and marginal probabilities. Preserve the original coordinate
indices when returning the queried complements. The implementation reports
floating-point feasibility and marginal repairs; its numerical solution
is not an outward-rounded proof certificate.

\subsection{A sharp separation from deterministic adaptive recovery}
\begin{proposition}[One-block randomization gap]
\label{prop:random-gap}
For one exact-total block with $E>0$, $m$ missing coordinates, and
$0\leq B\leq m-2$, put $r=m-B$ and
$h=\min_{|S|=r}d(S)$. Let $D_B=Eh$ be its optimal deterministic
worst-case width, which also equals the optimum over deterministic
adaptive policies. Then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{r}{m}D_B\leq R_B\leq D_B.
 \label{eq:random-gap}
\end{equation}
When $r$ divides $m$, the lower bound is attained by instances with
$D_B>0$, so the factor $m/r$ cannot be improved in general.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Pad every batch to $B$ queries. Each retains $r$ coordinates, and therefore
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^m\mathbb E_\pi v_i(Q)
 =r\mathbb E_\pi d(S(Q))\geq rh.
\]
Its largest expected coordinate coefficient is at least $rh/m$;
\eqref{eq:random-value} proves the lower bound. A point mass at the
deterministic optimal batch proves the upper bound. Equality of
deterministic batch and adaptive optima follows from the preceding
zero-answer argument.

For sharpness, form $m/r$ coefficient clusters, each containing $r$
equally spaced points of range $h>0$, and separate adjacent clusters
by gaps strictly greater than $h$. Every $r$-point subset has range
at least $h$, and a complete cluster attains it. Uniformly retain one
cluster and query its complement. Each coordinate then has expected
coefficient $hr/m$, giving $R_B=Ehr/m$.
\end{proof}

If $D_B=0$, both optima are zero and their ratio is undefined.
The ratio also compares the optimal randomized adaptive mean width with
the deterministic adaptive optimum, by the dual-completion result below. Every realized sharp comparison interval
contains the comparison value for the fixed record and for every completion
consistent with its query answers. The improvement is in worst expected width, not a bound
on every realized width, a higher certification probability, or measured
retrieval savings. This structured gap formula and compact implementation do not extend here
to overlapping or uncertain totals, physical caps, or heterogeneous query
costs. The general information theorem does allow such convex constraints
and fixed costs.


\subsection{Choosing among equally robust policies}\label{sec:tie-selection}
The primary minimax objective need not select a unique policy. Robust
optima can be dominated away from their worst case, a general issue studied
by Iancu and Trichakis~\citep{iancu2014}. For example, take one block with
$E=1$, $c=(0,2,4,12)$ and $B=2$. A policy retaining pairs
$\{1,2\},\{1,3\},\{2,3\}$ with probabilities $0.4,0.2,0.4$ has expected
coefficient vector $(1.6,1.6,1.6,0)$. Retaining $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4\}$
with probabilities $0.8,0.2$ instead gives $(1.6,1.6,1.6,1.6)$.
Both have optimal worst expected width $1.6$: dual weights
$(0.2,0.6,0.2,0)$ certify this value. The first policy is never worse,
and is strictly better when $y_4>0$.

Write $e_{gi}(\pi)=\mathbb E_\pi v_{gi}(Q)$. After finding $R_B$, a
target-independent secondary rule minimizes
\begin{equation}
 H(\pi)=\sum_g\frac{E_g}{m_g}\sum_{i\in\M_g} e_{gi}(\pi)
 \quad\text{subject to}\quad
 \sum_g E_g\max_i e_{gi}(\pi)\leq R_B.
 \label{eq:secondary-policy}
\end{equation}
This is expected width at the uniform-within-block feasible completion,
not an estimated load distribution. It is a linear objective on the
optimal face of the finite LP. A policy that is no worse at every feasible
record and strictly better somewhere would also improve $H$, contradicting
secondary optimality. Thus this standard refinement removes dominated
expected-width functions; it need not identify a unique distribution or
maximize certification.

We also check the stronger requirement of never worsening the archived
policy $\pi_0$. Expected-width dominance is equivalent to
$\sum_g E_g\max_i[e_{gi}(\pi)-e_{gi}(\pi_0)]\leq0$.
Block epigraph variables, free in sign, encode this condition linearly.
Minimizing $H$ over that set preserves a primary-optimal $\pi_0$'s robust
value in exact arithmetic. The two refinements answer different questions:
the first chooses a reference criterion on the optimal face; the second
only allows improvements over the particular saved policy. Neither uses
hidden targets or guarantees that certification improves.

\subsection{A hardest completion and randomized adaptive optimality}
\label{sec:adaptive-dual-completion}

Theorem~\ref{prop:risk_classification} already gives equality of randomized
batch and adaptive mean-width risks for this model. The specialization below
extracts the common hardest completion directly from the finite-game dual,
providing a computable optimality certificate for the compact solver.
Adaptive policies retain the same hard budget $B$, independent random seed,
exact coordinate information and sharp terminal interval as in
Section~\ref{sec:risk_setup}.

\begin{proposition}[A common hardest completion]
\label{prop:adaptive-dual-completion}
For the disjoint exact-total model, an optimum $w^*$ of
\eqref{eq:random-dual} defines the active-block values
$y^*_{gi}=w^*_{gi}$ of a feasible residual completion. Restore each omitted
constant-coefficient block with any fixed nonnegative allocation of its
total, and restore the determined singleton and zero-total values.
For every admissible terminal coordinate set $Q$,
\begin{equation}
 W(Q,y^*)\geq R_B.
 \label{eq:common-hardest-width}
\end{equation}
Consequently, if $\mathcal A_B$ is the class of randomized adaptive
coordinate-query policies satisfying the hard budget, then
\begin{equation}
 \inf_{A\in\mathcal A_B}\sup_{y\in\F}
       \mathbb E[W_A(y)] = R_B.
 \label{eq:adaptive-random-equality}
\end{equation}
The existing compact randomized batch policy attains this value. At $y^*$,
every execution of every admissible adaptive policy has terminal width at
least $R_B$, not merely an expectation bounded below by $R_B$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The dual equalities $\sum_i w^*_{gi}=E_g$ and inequalities $w^*_{gi}\geq0$
are exactly the active residual target constraints. Restoring the omitted
blocks gives a member of $\F$; appending the previously observed coordinates
gives a full record in $F$. Omitted blocks have zero uncertainty contribution.
Strong linear-programming
duality gives an optimal dual objective $\eta^*=R_B$; the batch inequalities
and the identity in \eqref{eq:random-objective} yield
$W(Q,y^*)=\sum_{g,i}w^*_{gi}v_{gi}(Q)\geq R_B$ for every $Q\in\mathcal Q_B$.
Querying the last remaining coordinate of a block, repeating a query, or
querying a coordinate already determined by the retained information adds
no constraint. Removing those redundant queries leaves an equivalent
terminal interval. Queries in omitted independent constant-coefficient
blocks can also be discarded for the width objective: their contrast
contribution is fixed, and their answers impose no constraint on other
blocks. The remaining set belongs to $\mathcal Q_B$ and costs no more.

By Proposition~\ref{prop:risk_concavity_transcript}, after fixing the seed,
the adaptive transcript has exactly the coordinate-observation fiber of
its terminal set $Q$. Its sharp width is therefore $W(Q,y)$; the query
choices and stopping decision add no constraints beyond those answers.

At $y^*$ the previous lower bound therefore applies to every terminal
transcript and seed. Taking expectations and then the worst completion
gives the lower bound $R_B$ for every adaptive policy. Randomized batches
are a subclass of adaptive policies, and the compact formulation constructs
a batch distribution with worst expected width $R_B$, proving equality.
\end{proof}

This argument uses established linear-programming duality and minimax
reasoning. Its consequence here is a checkable hardest-record certificate
for the existing structured optimizer. It does not optimize the expected
number of requests until certification, the probability that an interval
excludes zero, or the width on a particular realized historical record.

\paragraph{Extracting a completion from the implementation.}
The numerical compact implementation explicitly constrains $t_g\geq0$,
although these bounds are redundant in the mathematical primal. Its
nonnegative scenario multipliers may therefore satisfy
$\sum_i\widehat w_{gi}\leq E_g$ rather than equality. In exact arithmetic,
add each nonnegative mass deficit to a fixed coordinate. Since
$v_{gi}(Q)\geq0$, this cannot decrease any batch payoff. The resulting
completion is feasible, and its minimum batch payoff is both at least the
dual value and at most the primal value; strong duality makes these equal.
The verification code retains the raw multipliers and records clipping,
any tiny excess-mass correction, and the added deficits. It checks the
completed record with the existing weighted pricing oracle and, on small
instances, by independently enumerating every final coordinate subset
of size at most $B$, including redundant full-block queries. Numerical
discrepancies are reported separately from the exact theorem.

\begin{proposition}[Two best-side splits for an arbitrary block]
Let $n\geq3$, $E>0$, $x\geq0$, $\sum_{h=1}^n x_h=E$, and let the target be
$d+\sum_hc_hx_h$. Each query returns one internal prefix sum. Write
$r(p,q)=\max_{p<h\leq q}c_h-\min_{p<h\leq q}c_h$.
For first boundary $k$, put $\ell_k=r(0,k)$ and $r_k=r(k,n)$.
Define each of the following quantities whenever its corresponding
side has an eligible second boundary:
\[
 \alpha_k=\min_{1\leq j<k}\max\{r(0,j),r(j,k)\},\qquad
 \beta_k=\min_{k<j<n}\max\{r(k,j),r(j,n)\}.
\]
Choose a minimizing boundary on each side, breaking ties by smallest index.
When both sides are nonempty, for normalized first answer $t=s_k/E$,
the optimal conditional worst width is
\[
 E\min\{\alpha_k t+r_k(1-t),\ \ell_k t+\beta_k(1-t)\}.
\]
Thus only one best boundary on each side is needed, independently of $t$.
If $\Delta_k=\ell_k+r_k-\alpha_k-\beta_k>0$, the switch is
\[
 t_k^\star=\frac{r_k-\beta_k}{\Delta_k}\in[0,1],
\]
and the worst width for this first boundary is
\[
 A_k=E\max\left\{\alpha_k,\beta_k,
          \frac{\ell_kr_k-\alpha_k\beta_k}{\Delta_k}\right\}.
\]
Use the best right split below the switch and the best left split above
it; either is valid at equality. If $\Delta_k=0$, the two lines coincide
and $A_k=E\max\{\ell_k,r_k\}$. At $k=1$ only the right split exists and
$A_1=E\beta_1$; at $k=n-1$ only the left split exists and
$A_{n-1}=E\alpha_{n-1}$. The optimal deterministic two-query guarantee is
$\min_k A_k$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
All second queries left of $k$ have the common right-side contribution
$r_k(1-t)$; minimizing their remaining term $t$ times a split range
selects the same minimizing boundary for every $t\geq0$. The right-side
argument is identical. Moreover $\alpha_k\leq\ell_k$ and
$\beta_k\leq r_k$. Hence $\Delta_k\geq0$, and when it is positive the
displayed intersection belongs to $[0,1]$. The maximum of the lower
envelope occurs at an endpoint or that intersection. Its endpoint
values are $\beta_k,\alpha_k$, and its intersection value is the displayed
quotient. If $\Delta_k=0$, both nonnegative differences vanish, so the
lines coincide. At an outer eligible boundary the single-hour side has
zero coefficient range, giving the stated one-line values.
\end{proof}

All contiguous ranges can be built in $O(n^2)$ arithmetic operations
and stored in $O(n^2)$ space. Scanning the candidates for every
$\alpha_k,\beta_k$ costs another $O(n^2)$ operations; the closed forms
then cost $O(n)$. This is an arithmetic-operation bound for the exact-total,
uncapped two-prefix problem, not a bit-complexity or noisy-polytope claim.
For $n=2$, the single internal register determines both increments;
for $n=1$ the total already determines the only increment.

\paragraph{Uniform-cap verification oracle.}
For the separate sensitivity scenarios $0\leq x_h\leq u$, a segment
containing sorted coefficients $a_1\leq\cdots\leq a_m$ has a sharp-width
function of its retained mass $s$ with slopes
\[
 a_m-a_1,\ a_{m-1}-a_2,\ldots,\ a_1-a_m,
\]
each over a mass interval of length $u$. These slopes are nonincreasing.
For a collection of segments with an unknown allocation of a fixed total
mass, merge their slope lists in decreasing order and integrate to that
mass. This computes the largest sum of their sharp widths: allocating
mass to the greatest available marginal slope respects each segment's
precedence because its slopes are already decreasing.

For a first prefix of mass $s$, the conditional worst width of a second
query is the merged-left value at $s$ plus the merged-right value at
$E-s$. It is concave and piecewise affine, with breakpoints at multiples
of $u$ and at $E$ minus those multiples, restricted to the feasible
first-answer interval
$[\max(0,E-(n-k)u),\min(E,ku)]$.
On each common breakpoint interval, compute the lower affine envelope
over second queries, including intersections, and maximize it. This gives
an exact rational oracle when the inputs are rational. It independently
checks the generic endpoint LP; it does not reuse the uncapped two-line
formula under caps.

pointwise-dominated optima up to numerical tolerances. It is not labeled precision-optimal.

\paragraph{The distribution and exact optimality of the batch bound.}

percentile and maximum of the rational adverse-profile bounds are all
exactly $1/2$. All 290 equal $1/2$; none is below $1/2$, $0.4$, $0.3$
or $0.2$. This is exact equality, not rounding of nearby LP solutions.
Corollary~\ref{prop:counter-half-floor} explains why the lower tail
cannot extend below $1/2$ when a deterministic two-register policy
guarantees the precision. For each case, a constructed equal mixture of
two batches succeeds with probability at least $1/2$ on \emph{every}
completion. The independently checked finite adverse profiles give the
opposite inequality for every randomized batch. Consequently,
\[
 \underbrace{S_B^\star\geq\tfrac12}_{\text{two-batch cover of every completion}}
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 \underbrace{S_B^\star\leq\tfrac12}_{\text{finite adverse-profile certificate}}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_B^\star=\tfrac12.
\]
The finite profile set is sufficient for the upper bound; the lower bound
comes from the continuum covering proof, not from testing those profiles.
Thus the 290 cases attain the largest possible precision-success gap
between guaranteed deterministic adaptation and a randomized batch under
this two-register structure. This sharp guarantee does not assert a
similarly large gap on the recorded responses.

\section{A bounded-error extension and its interpretation}\label{app:stored-error}
Let $m_h$ be accumulated meter increments, $x_h$ latent physical increments,
and $r_0,r_6$ stored outer readings, with $E_{\mathrm{obs}}=r_6-r_0$.
A declared conditional relative-increment allowance $0\leq\alpha<1$ and
finite storage-error bounds $\delta_k\geq0$ define the following
polytope, assumed nonempty. It is compact because the upper total bound
bounds the nonnegative increments and all error coordinates are bounded:
\[
\begin{gathered}
 x_h,m_h\geq0,\quad(1-\alpha)x_h\leq m_h\leq(1+\alpha)x_h,\quad
 |b|\leq\delta_0,\quad |e_k|\leq\delta_k,\\
 E_{\mathrm{obs}}-\delta_6\leq b+\sum_hm_h\leq E_{\mathrm{obs}}+\delta_6.
\end{gathered}
\]
An internal query returns the exact stored readout
$b+\sum_{h\leq k}m_h+e_k=r_k-r_0$, and the target remains $d+c^\top x$.
Thus fixed stored errors can be coordinates of the latent record, fixed
before algorithmic randomization, and the convex exact-linear-query
results apply to this enlarged model. The five first-query LPs have
146 variables each, or 98 when $\alpha=0$ permits identifying $m=x$.

This is a sensitivity model, not a verified meter specification.
An accuracy class does not by itself justify applying $\alpha$ to every
hour, and it is not a relative error bound on a lifetime cumulative register.
Exact float quantization has a nonconvex graph; a bounded-error polytope
is a conservative outer model, with optimality only for that model.
Actual stored values can be replayed, but they do not reveal latent
physical truth. The original coefficient offset must be retained:
subtracting $\min_hc_h$ changes the target uncertainty when its total
is no longer fixed.

Eighteen constructed prototype cases check exact limits, bounded-error
examples and coupled polytopes. Their scalar-first two-successor values
agree with the full successor family within $4.0\times10^{-15}$.
Separate numerical checks include zero-width cases, latent-record feasibility
and extracted-policy upper values, with maximum discrepancy
$8.89\times10^{-15}$. These are floating-point within-study checks using
$10^{-9}$ LP tolerances, not external-meter validation or human specialist
confirmation. The tested nonzero-error settings destroy the half-width guarantee in the
constructed example lying exactly at the threshold, so empirical
robustness must be evaluated rather than inferred.

\section{S8. Shared-query certificates and protocol selection}
\section{Shared observations and joint local width}
\label{app:joint-width-boundary}
Vector-valued recovery and the possible value of adaptive information are
established subjects \citep{krieg2025}. The result here concerns a particular
shared-query objective and interface; it is not a general novelty claim for
vector recovery.

Let $T_r(y)$, $r=1,\ldots,m$, be affine contrasts on the same feasible set,
with positive initial widths $W_{r,0}$. After one shared transcript $Q$, define
\[
 J_Q(y)=\max_r\frac{W_{r,Q}(y)}{W_{r,0}},\qquad
 R_{\mathcal C}^{J}=\inf_{P\in\mathcal C}\sup_y
                  \mathbb E_\omega J_{Q_P(y,\omega)}(y).
\]
This is the weighted $\ell_\infty$ local diameter of the vector-valued
information fiber. Its mean risk is distinct from the probability of
attaining a precision threshold. Zero-width contrasts can be omitted;
an audit with none remaining needs no query. The maximum of the individual
concave widths need not be concave, so the scalar classification does not
supply a no-adaptation result for $J$.

\paragraph{An exact two-prefix separation.}
Let $F=\{z\geq0:\sum_{h=1}^6z_h=1\}$, allow the five internal prefix
queries, and impose two queries total. Use
\[
 c^{(1)}=(1/2,0,1,1/2,1/2,0),\qquad
 c^{(2)}=(2/3,2/3,1,1/3,0,1).
\]
Both initial widths equal one. Segment masses times coefficient ranges give
the sharp scalar widths. Query prefix~3 first, with answer $t$. The
conditional worst joint widths for second queries~5 and~2 are
\[
 G_{35}(t)=\max(t,1/3),\qquad
 G_{32}(t)=\max(1/2,1-t).
\]
Choose~5 for $t\leq1/2$ and~2 otherwise; the joint width is at most $1/2$
for every record. At the single record $z^*=(0,0,1/2,0,0,1/2)$, the ten
batch widths, in order $12,13,14,15,23,24,25,34,35,45$, are
\[
 (1,3/4,5/6,1/2,1/2,5/6,1/2,3/4,1/2,1/2).
\]
Here $ij$ denotes boundaries $\{i,j\}$. Fixing an adaptive seed, equal
queried answers reproduce the complete query sequence and stopping decision.
Its terminal fiber is therefore that of its final set. Every adaptive seed
has width at least $1/2$ at this same record; fewer queries cannot reduce
width. Hence $R_{\rm adaptive}^{J}=1/2$.

The batch mixture with probabilities $1/6,1/3,1/2$ on $12,23,35$ has expected
joint widths $(2/3,2/3,2/3,2/3,2/3,1/2)$ at $e_1,\ldots,e_6$.
For each batch, scalar widths are linear in $z$, so $J_Q$ and its mixture
expectation are convex. These vertex values bound the expectation throughout
$F$ by $2/3$. Conversely, adverse masses $(1/3,0,1/6,1/6,1/3,0)$ at those
vertices give the ten batches expected widths
\[
 (2/3,2/3,2/3,2/3,2/3,13/18,5/6,5/6,2/3,2/3).
\]
Every batch mixture therefore has worst expected width at least $2/3$:
\[
 \boxed{R_{\rm adaptive}^{J}=1/2<2/3=R_{\rm batch}^{J}.}
\]
These are constructed mean-risk values, not empirical performance or
success probabilities. The contrasts can share a common forecast baseline:
$a=0$ and $b^{(r)}=3c^{(r)}$ realize their coefficients in six-target paired
MSE; constants do not affect widths. Adding other comparisons changes $J$.

\paragraph{Specialized exact algorithms.}
For any finite contrast family on this exact-total prefix simplex, form
$V_{hQ}=J_Q(e_h)$. Convexity proves that the finite-game LP
\[
 \min_{\pi\geq0,\,\sum_Q\pi_Q=1,v}v
 \quad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_Q\pi_QV_{hQ}\leq v\quad(h=1,\ldots,n)
\]
computes the exact randomized-batch mean value. This vertex argument does
not apply to the indicator of precision failure.

For deterministic adaptive two-query planning, each contrast's conditional
worst normalized width $g_{kjr}(t)$ is affine in first-prefix mass $t$.
Maximizing over records and the finite contrast family commutes, so
$G_{kj}(t)=\max_r g_{kjr}(t)$ is the conditional worst joint width. The optimum is
\[
 A_{\rm det}=\min_k\max_{t\in[0,1]}\min_{j\ne k}G_{kj}(t).
\]
Enumerate $0,1$ and every in-range intersection of the underlying affine
lines, including lines from different second-query choices. Between
consecutive intersections their ordering is fixed; the resulting
minimum of group maxima is affine there. Its maximum occurs at an endpoint.
This gives an exact rational procedure with finite index tie-breaking.
It computes deterministic adaptive worst width, not the general randomized
adaptive optimum. Caps, noisy readings or another query dictionary require
new conditional calculations. The accompanying checker reconstructs all
example certificates with rational arithmetic; finite checks supplement
the continuum arguments above.

\subsection{Shared-budget audit: complete computational protocol}
\label{sec:joint_shared_protocol}
The post-analysis shared-query evaluation uses every block in the existing
original (819 blocks) and external (279 blocks) panels. Each block retains the
six comparisons induced by its four frozen forecast vectors. All six initial
widths are positive; the implementation verifies this and the six-pair count
rather than selecting contrasts or removing unfavorable blocks. The panel
unit remains a meter series, not an independent model-pair observation. The
three systems within each panel and their repeated days are dependent.

For a nonnegative increment vector $y$ of total $E$, pair $r$ has affine
contrast coefficients $c_r$. We normalize by the original width
$W_{r,0}=E(\max_hc_{rh}-\min_hc_{rh})$. Subtracting the smallest coefficient
and dividing by its range makes the normalized coefficient range one; this
does not change a normalized width because the retained total is fixed.
The common precision target is $\max_r W_r/W_{r,0}\leq1/2$. A query returns
one internal cumulative register, and two distinct queried registers are
shared by all six comparisons. The model charges two scalar readings;
adaptive acquisition has two information rounds and a batch has one. This
does not represent two whole-trace exports or measured acquisition latency.

\paragraph{Exact-total planning.}
For each of the ten two-register batches $Q$, the normalized pair widths
are linear in $y/E$. Their maximum is convex. Thus the worst expected joint
width of a fixed batch mixture is attained at one of the six concentration
profiles, and the ten-action/six-profile game is an exact formulation.
The dual profile distribution and the batch mixture are retained. For a
first boundary $k$ and answer $t$, each possible second boundary $j$ has
conditional worst pair widths $a_{kjr}t+b_{kjr}$.
The conditional joint objective is $\max_r(a_{kjr}t+b_{kjr})$ and the
adaptive continuation minimizes it over $j$. Every intersection of these
lines, together with the feasible endpoints, is evaluated; the minimum over
first boundaries is the deterministic adaptive optimum. This line arrangement
does not assume that the maximum across pair widths is concave. No optimality
among randomized adaptive policies is inferred from this computation.

All policies are saved before the program opens the fine-resolution response
CSV. The deterministic batch first minimizes its worst joint width and then
its uniform concentration-profile mean. The greedy first boundary minimizes
one-query worst joint width, with the best achievable two-query continuation
as its tie-breaker; the second query is conditionally optimized. This gives
the greedy comparator a strong continuation, rather than an arbitrary second
reading. Both primary and secondary numerical ties use $10^{-12}$ and then
the earliest index. The randomized batch uses the uniform profile mean as a
secondary LP objective on a face allowing $10^{-10}$ above the primary
optimum. An additional outcome-informed diagnostic minimizes and maximizes
realized width and precision success over a $10^{-9}$ primary-optimum face.
Those extrema are diagnostic oracles, not deployed policies. In the external
panel their mean success range is below $3\times10^{-8}$, so the observed
comparison is not materially explained by this LP tie choice. The numerical
LP feasibility tolerance is $10^{-9}$ and comparison tolerance $10^{-8}$.

\paragraph{Capacity sensitivity.}
The illustrative constraints $0\leq y_h\leq uE$, with $u=1/2$ or $1/3$,
are supplied to every policy. The threshold remains half the original
uncapped width. For sorted coefficients $d_1\leq\cdots\leq d_m$ in one
segment, its sharp width as a function of retained mass has consecutive
marginal slopes $d_m-d_1,d_{m-1}-d_2,\ldots$, each lasting mass $uE$.
For two segments whose combined mass is known, the worst sum of widths is
their concave max-convolution: concatenate and sort these marginal slopes.
Consequently each conditional pair-width function is piecewise affine with
known capacity knots. The same line-arrangement method is applied separately
between consecutive knots. Deterministic fixed-batch worst widths follow
from the corresponding convolution over all segments. Direct endpoint LPs
independently verify the formulas. The simplex-vertex randomized formulation
is not reused as an exact capped randomized solver.

Recorded cap compatibility is checked using exact decimal/integer source
arithmetic. Incompatible records remain in the case ledger and have no
claimed cap-based replay outcome. Counts of hypothetical capped geometries,
compatible records, guarantees and recorded successes are reported separately.
At a cap of $E/2$, for example, only 200 of 279 external records are compatible.
This cap is not an inferred device limit or an outcome-selected cohort rule.

\paragraph{Stored-readout sensitivity.}
The separate noisy-policy certificates fix a coefficient-derived branching
policy before evaluating any responses. The latent model allows each queried
internal register an error in $[-0.01E,0.01E]$, while endpoints remain exact.
For replay, the observed answer is the actual stored register: sharp intervals
are recomputed over every compatible record, rather than adding a simulated
noise draw. The noisy adaptive policy is not claimed globally optimal.
The fixed-batch comparator is selected from all ten robust batch bounds before
answers. A further control freezes the capacity-specific noiseless policy
before applying the same error allowance. Exact rational dual-residual
certificates check the guarantees separately from floating-point replay.
In that capacity-aware variant, the original greedy policy remains a frozen
uncapped control; the main combined-sensitivity comparison is therefore with
the robust best fixed batch, not a claim of superiority over every cap-aware
greedy rule.

\subsection{Shared certificates with bounded stored-register errors}
\label{sec:joint-stored-errors}
This sensitivity calculation retains the same two shared prefix queries and
the original precision target $J\leq 1/2$.  Let $x_h=y_h/E$, so that
$x\geq0$ and $\sum_hx_h=1$, and let $P_kx=\sum_{h\leq k}x_h$.
The six normalized contrasts are obtained by subtracting each contrast's
minimum coefficient and dividing by its coefficient range.  Hence $J$ is
the largest of the six sharp widths divided by its original, uncapped,
zero-query width.  Optional capacity information imposes $x_h\leq\alpha$;
it does not change this denominator.

The stored answer at boundary $k$ is $z_k=P_kx+e_k$, where the five errors
are fixed before the policy's random choices and $|e_k|\leq\delta$.
Endpoints remain exact.  We use $\delta=0.01$, with $\alpha=1$ or $1/2$.
These are declared uncertainty sets, not measured device specifications.
The first query and piecewise second-query rule are frozen from the
coefficient-only noiseless planner.  A first answer outside its noiseless
range uses the nearest endpoint action.  At a shared boundary the first
matching closed interval is used.  We separately evaluate the original
planner and the already computed capacity-aware planner; neither is claimed
optimal for the noisy problem.

\paragraph{An all-record upper certificate.}
For a branch that queries $k$ first and $j$ second when the first answer
belongs to $[l,u]$, solve, for every normalized contrast $c_r$,
\[
 \max c_r^\top(v-w)\quad\text{subject to}\quad
 \begin{cases}
 v,w\geq0,\quad\boldsymbol1^\top v=\boldsymbol1^\top w=1,
       \quad v_h,w_h\leq\alpha,\\
 l\leq t\leq u,\quad |P_kv-t|\leq\delta,
       \quad |P_kw-t|\leq\delta,\\
 |P_jv-P_jw|\leq2\delta.
 \end{cases}
\]
The last inequality is exactly the condition that the two possible second
answers have overlapping error intervals.  Thus every terminal pair of
compatible records is included.  Maximizing these bounds over branches and
contrasts certifies the frozen policy for every allowed record and error
vector.  Closed branch endpoints can overcover a tie but cannot invalidate
the upper bound.  A fixed batch uses the analogous two-prefix constraints,
without a branching variable; all ten batches are evaluated.

\paragraph{A bound against every randomized batch.}
A feasible endpoint pair $v,w$ for batch $Q$ with joint contrast difference
strictly exceeding $1/2$ supplies one adverse record: choose actual profile
$v$, set $e_q=(P_qw-P_qv)/2$ for $q\in Q$, and set the other errors to zero.
This complete latent record is fixed independently of the batch selected
later.  Compatible endpoint alternatives certify additional failures of
other batches at the same record.  Unverified entries are conservatively
counted as success.  For any rational distribution $\lambda$ on these
records, the maximum, over the ten batches, of the resulting expected
success upper bounds also bounds the worst-case success probability of
every randomized batch.  This requires neither vertex sufficiency nor a
claim of equality with the full-continuum optimum.

\paragraph{Numerical certification.}
The checked objectives use exact rational normalization of the original
saved binary64 contrast coefficients.  Floating-point LP multipliers are
converted to rationals, their signs are enforced, and any stationarity
residual is bounded over the finite variable box.  This gives rigorous
upper bounds without assuming exact floating-point dual feasibility.
Specifically, for a minimization LP $\min f^\top z$ with $Az\leq b$,
$Hz=h$ and $L\leq z\leq U$, any rational $\lambda\leq0$ and unrestricted
$\nu$ give the lower bound
\[
 \lambda^\top b+\nu^\top h+
 \sum_i\min\{r_iL_i,r_iU_i\},\qquad
 r=f-A^\top\lambda-H^\top\nu.
\]
Indeed $\lambda^\top Az\geq\lambda^\top b$ and each residual term is
bounded over its interval.  Negating this bound for
$f^\top z=-c_r^\top(v-w)$ yields the exported width upper certificate.
Rational feasible endpoints give lower bounds.  A separate solver-free
checker verifies source hashes, coefficients, every reported precision
status, all policy branches, and the fixed adverse mixtures.  These checks
certify the declared mathematical instances; they do not establish physical
sensor accuracy or field savings.
\subsection{An objective-matched randomized batch challenger}
\label{sec:joint-precision-challenger}
The following calculation addresses precision success, not expected width.
It uses the uncapped exact-total simplex and the original threshold $1/2$;
the bounded-error calculations above define a different information model.
Write
\[
 J_Q(x)=\max_{r=1,\ldots,6}v_{Qr}^{\top}x,
 \qquad x\geq0,\quad\boldsymbol1^\top x=1,
\]
where $v_{Qr}$ contains the normalized coefficient range within each segment
created by batch $Q$.  These ten batches use the same two prefix queries as
the adaptive policy.  A deterministic batch guarantees precision exactly
when every entry of every $v_{Qr}$ is at most $1/2$.

\paragraph{Full-simplex pair-cover certificate.}
For two batches $Q,R$, suppose that for every contrast pair $(r,s)$ a
number $\lambda_{rs}\in[0,1]$ satisfies
\[
 \lambda_{rs}v_{Qr,i}+(1-\lambda_{rs})v_{Rs,i}
 \leq\tfrac12\qquad(i=1,\ldots,6).
\]
Then $J_Q(x)$ and $J_R(x)$ cannot both exceed $1/2$.  Otherwise some $r,s$
would have both linear values strictly above $1/2$, and their indicated
convex combination would be above $1/2$.  Summing the displayed coordinate
inequalities with weights $x_i$ gives the opposite conclusion.  Consequently,
the equal mixture of $Q,R$ succeeds with probability at least $1/2$ on
\emph{every} feasible record.  This includes threshold equality as success.
Each multiplier is obtained exactly by intersecting six rational interval
constraints on $\lambda$; no discretization of records is used.

\paragraph{Full-domain upper bounds from finitely many records.}
We use the six simplex vertices, their 15 pairwise midpoints, and the
uniform record.  Define $A_{zQ}=\boldsymbol1\{J_Q(z)\leq1/2\}$.
For any rational probability distribution $\mu$ on these 22 feasible
records, let $U=\max_Q\sum_z\mu_z A_{zQ}$.  Every randomized batch policy
has some feasible record with success probability at most $U$: its
average success over $\mu$ is at most $U$, so its minimum cannot exceed
that average.  This is the usual finite-game weak-duality argument, not
a claim that these records exhaust the continuum.  The numerical game
solver supplies candidate weights; rational normalization and direct
evaluation of all ten expectations supply the actual checked upper bound.
If the pair-cover guarantee and this upper bound both equal $1/2$, the
full-continuum randomized-batch optimum is therefore exactly $1/2$.
Elsewhere the interval between the lower and upper bound is retained.

\paragraph{Frozen comparison policy.}
Before replaying answers, we first choose a precision-guaranteeing
deterministic batch if one exists.  Otherwise we choose a certified equal
pair if one exists.  Ties minimize worst expected joint width over the six
vertices, then its uniform vertex average, then the batch indices.  If no
pair certificate exists, we retain the saved mean-width mixture and assign
only the trivial precision lower bound zero.  These fallback policies are
not described as precision-optimal.  Selection uses the frozen coefficients
only, with unchanged normalization and budget.

All 36 multipliers for every selected pair and the feasible adverse
weights are exported.  A solver-free rational checker reconstructs the
coefficients from the hashed source rows, verifies all coordinate
inequalities, recomputes every finite-record success indicator, and checks
the lower and upper bounds.  For the complete panels these bounds match
on 468 of 819 original and 212 of 279 external blocks.  The matching value
is $1/2$ on 165 and 57 blocks, respectively; the remaining matches have
value zero or one.  No continuum optimality is inferred for the unmatched
blocks, nor are these exact-total guarantees transferred to the
bounded-error sensitivity models.
\paragraph{Selecting the acquisition protocol.}
The interface, budget unit and operational preference must be specified before
an acquisition guarantee is used. A reference implementation selects among
the supplied fixed, adaptive and full-range certificates for the 29 cases.
It checks permitted
queries, scalar-reading and information-round caps, transport grouping, and
the requested preference; a missing guarantee triggers escalation rather than
a relaxed target. This is an integration of the preceding results, not a new
optimization theorem. A frozen offline evaluation retains all 29 external

\subsection{Growing scalar-family separation: complete proof}
\label{sec:risk_growing}
For $K\ge2$, let $a_1,\ldots,a_K\ge0$ sum to one and impose
$0\le b_j\le a_j$. The actual queryable coordinates are
$a_1,\ldots,a_{K-1},b_1,\ldots,b_K$; $a_K$ is determined by the
first $K-1$ capacities. Define
\begin{equation}
 T=\sum_{i<K}a_i+\sum_{j=1}^K b_j,\qquad B=2K-2.
 \label{eq:risk_K_setup}
\end{equation}
This is a compact convex polytope in $2K-1$ dimensions, and every
coordinate has the same unit cost.

\begin{theorem}[Sharp growing separations]
\label{prop:risk_K_family}
For~\eqref{eq:risk_K_setup}, the optimal worst expected width is $1/K$
for both randomized batch and adaptive policies. For every $p>1$,
\begin{equation}
 R_{\mathrm{batch}}^{w^p}=1/K,\qquad
 R_{\mathrm{adaptive}}^{w^p}=1/K^p.
 \label{eq:risk_K_moments}
\end{equation}
For required width $\tau=1/K$, the best worst-case success probabilities
are $1/K$ for randomized batches and $1$ for adaptive policies.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
It suffices to consider batches using the full budget: adding observations
cannot increase width. Such a batch omits exactly one coordinate.
Omitting $b_j$ leaves width $a_j$; omitting $a_i$, $i<K$, leaves width
$a_i+a_K-b_i-b_K$. The latter follows because the remaining feasible
range of $a_i$ is $[b_i,a_i+a_K-b_K]$ and its coefficient in $T$ is one.
An adaptive policy queries all explicit capacities and then all $b_j$
except one having the smallest capacity. Its width is
$\min_j a_j\le1/K$. At the record with all $a_j=1/K$ and all $b_j=0$,
every full-budget batch has width at least $1/K$. This proves the
adaptive lower bounds and the mean-width lower bound.

The randomized batch policy omitting each $b_j$ with probability $1/K$
has expected width exactly $1/K$. Its expected $p$th power is
$K^{-1}\sum_j a_j^p\le1/K$, since $0\le a_j\le1$ and $p>1$.
For the reverse moment bound, average over the $K$ records
$a=e_j$, $b=0$. A batch omitting a $b_j$ has average $p$th-power width
$1/K$; one omitting an explicit $a_i$ has average $2/K$.
Therefore every batch distribution has worst-case moment at least $1/K$.

For precision, omitting a uniformly random $b_j$ succeeds with
probability at least $1/K$ at every record, because at least one capacity
is no greater than $1/K$. To prove the matching upper bound, use $K$
records indexed by $j$, with $b=0$,
\[
 a_j=\frac{1}{2K},\qquad
 a_i=\frac{2K-1}{2K(K-1)}\quad(i\ne j).
\]
These capacities sum to one. Only the batch omitting $b_j$ reaches
width at most $1/K$ at record $j$: every other $b$-omitting batch has larger
width, and every $a$-omitting batch has width $a_i+a_K>1/K$.
If $q_j$ is the probability of omitting $b_j$, worst-case success is
at most $\min_j q_j\le1/K$. The adaptive policy always succeeds.
The precision upper bound uses these interior capacity records;
checking only vertices would not establish it.
\end{proof}

The moment ratio is $K^{p-1}$, while the ratio of worst-case success
probabilities at precision $1/K$ is $K$. These separations use a
structured constructed feasible set and a budget querying all but one
coordinate. They establish the possible difference between objectives;
they do not establish comparable gains at small relative budgets in a
deployment or in the historical forecasting study.
\section{S10. Computing policies and hardest records on polytopes}\label{sec:general-algorithms}
Let $F=\{y\in\mathbb R^n:Ay\le b\}$ be nonempty and compact, and let
$T(y)=c^\top y+d$. Equalities can be represented by paired inequalities.
The constant $d$ does not affect width. Write $\rho=\max_{y\in F}\min_{Q\in\mathcal Q}W_Q(y)$ for the hardest-record value, equal to the optimal randomized worst expected width by the scalar theorem in S11. The first formulation allows any
declared finite family $\mathcal Q$ of legal batches; $L_Q$ denotes the matrix
of their exact linear measurements.

\subsection{An enumerative hardest-record formulation}
Introduce a common record $y$, endpoint witnesses $u_Q,v_Q$ for each
$Q\in\mathcal Q$, and a scalar $\eta$. Consider
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general-hardest-lp}
\begin{aligned}
\max\quad &\eta\\
\text{subject to}\quad &Ay\le b,\quad Au_Q\le b,\quad Av_Q\le b
                         &&(Q\in\mathcal Q),\\
&L_Qu_Q=L_Qy,\quad L_Qv_Q=L_Qy &&(Q\in\mathcal Q),\\
&\eta\le c^\top(u_Q-v_Q) &&(Q\in\mathcal Q).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For fixed $y$, the independent witnesses for batch $Q$ can attain its sharp
width $W_Q(y)$. Thus the largest feasible $\eta$ at that record is
$\min_Q W_Q(y)$, and the optimum of \eqref{eq:general-hardest-lp} is $\rho$.
Its common record is a certificate that every legal batch leaves width at
least $\rho$ there.

The nonnegative dual multipliers $\pi_Q$ of the last constraints form an
optimal randomized batch policy. Indeed, the free variable $\eta$ forces
$\sum_Q\pi_Q=1$. Dualizing these constraints first yields
\[
\min_{\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal Q)}
 \max_{y,u_Q,v_Q}\sum_Q\pi_Qc^\top(u_Q-v_Q)
 =\min_{\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal Q)}\max_{y\in F}
       \sum_Q\pi_Q W_Q(y)=\rho,
\]
where the endpoint feasibility constraints remain in the inner maximization.
Ordinary linear-programming strong duality supplies the equality and the
policy. In the implementation, a separate
LP fixes the extracted $\pi$ and maximizes its expected width, checking the
upper bound independently of the constraints involving $\eta$.

With $M=|\mathcal Q|$ batches, the formulation has $n(1+2M)+1$ variables.
It is polynomial in the explicitly enumerated batch family, which may be
exponential in the number of available measurements. It is a small-instance
benchmark and certificate generator; the aggregate-specific compact
formulation avoids this enumeration in its structured setting.

\subsection{An exact deterministic two-query algorithm}\label{sec:two-query}
Assume every one of the $n\ge2$ coordinates is queryable at unit cost and the
hard budget is two. An unused query can be added without increasing width,
so considering two distinct coordinates includes policies that stop earlier.
For a first query $i$ and feasible answer $t$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conditional-two-query}
g_{ij}(t)=\max\{c^\top(u-v):Au\le b,\ Av\le b,\
                u_i=v_i=t,\ u_j=v_j\},\qquad j\ne i.
\end{equation}
This maximizes the terminal width over all feasible records having first
answer $t$, after second query $j$. The best deterministic policy beginning
with $i$ therefore has worst width
$\rho_i=\max_t\min_{j\ne i}g_{ij}(t)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:two-query-lp}
For each $i$, $\rho_i$ is the value of the following single linear program,
with one endpoint pair $(u^j,v^j)$ for each $j\ne i$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-query-lp}
\begin{aligned}
\max\quad &\eta\\
\text{subject to}\quad &Au^j\le b,\quad Av^j\le b &&(j\ne i),\\
&(u^j)_i=(v^j)_i=t,\quad (u^j)_j=(v^j)_j &&(j\ne i),\\
&\eta\le c^\top(u^j-v^j) &&(j\ne i).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The optimal deterministic adaptive two-query worst-width value is
$\min_i\rho_i$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At fixed feasible $t$, each endpoint pair has only its own constraints and
can attain $g_{ij}(t)$. All inequalities on $\eta$ are feasible precisely up
to their smallest attainable upper bound, $\min_{j\ne i}g_{ij}(t)$.
Maximizing over the shared $t$ proves the first claim.
For any deterministic policy beginning with $i$, its chosen second coordinate
at answer $t$ has conditional worst width at least $\min_jg_{ij}(t)$.
Conversely, choosing a minimizing $j$ after observing $t$ attains that value.
Finite tie breaking gives a measurable selection because these polyhedral
value functions are piecewise linear on their feasible interval. Finally,
minimize over the first coordinate.
\end{proof}

For rational input, $n$ LPs of polynomial description size choose the first
coordinate; after its answer, $n-1$ conditional LPs implement the second
choice. This is a polynomial-time deterministic two-query procedure in the
standard bit-complexity model, including the encoding length of rational
query answers. For arbitrary exact answers, the policy is still defined by
the conditional LPs; the polynomial bit-complexity statement applies to
rationally encoded inputs and answers. It does not solve arbitrary-budget
randomized precision-risk optimization or minimize expected requests until
certification. No grid of possible first answers is used.
\section{S11. Scalar penalty classification and full information proofs}
\section{Information, terminal width, and risk}
\label{sec:risk_setup}
Let $F\subset\mathbb R^n$ be nonempty, compact, and convex, and let
$T(y)=c^\top y+d$ be the scalar quantity to be audited. A fixed finite
collection of exact linear measurements is available. Write $L_Q$ for
the measurement matrix of a selected set $Q$, and let $\mathcal Q$ be
the nonempty finite family of admissible final sets, available to both
policy classes. Admissibility is determined before observing answers; for example,
$\sum_{j\in Q}k_j\le B$ for nonnegative fixed costs $k_j$.
Coordinate observations correspond to rows of the identity matrix.
The results do not require nonnegative targets or disjoint aggregates.

For a feasible record $y$, define the information fiber and its sharp
target width by
\begin{equation}
 F_Q(y)=\{z\in F:L_Qz=L_Qy\},\qquad
 W_Q(y)=\max_{z\in F_Q(y)}T(z)-\min_{z\in F_Q(y)}T(z).
 \label{eq:risk_fiber}
\end{equation}
The extrema exist by compactness, and $0\le W_Q(y)\le D_T$, where
$D_T=\max_F T-\min_F T$. This is the local diameter of the
scalar information fiber. The optimized quantity is the full fiber's
diameter, rather than the actual error of a selected point estimate.
An additive constant in $T$ does
not affect $W_Q$. In particular, a paired squared-loss contrast is
affine in the unknown verification targets and fits this formulation.

A randomized batch policy draws $Q\sim\pi$ before seeing answers. An
adaptive policy selects successive measurements and a stopping decision
from the answers already observed and an independent internal random
seed. Every execution must terminate after finitely many queries at a
set in $\mathcal Q$. Query and stopping rules are jointly measurable in
the observed history and seed; observations are exact. For a finite-valued,
nondecreasing function $\phi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$, define
\begin{equation}
 R_{\mathrm{batch}}^{\phi}
 =\inf_{\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal Q)}\sup_{y\in F}
   \mathbb E_{Q\sim\pi}\phi(W_Q(y)),\qquad
 R_{\mathrm{adaptive}}^{\phi}
 =\inf_{\mathcal P}\sup_{y\in F}
   \mathbb E\phi(W_{Q_{\mathcal P}(y)}(y)).
 \label{eq:risk_objectives}
\end{equation}
The hidden record is fixed before the random seed is drawn. Thus the
supremum is outside the expectation. When measurement error is represented
by additional hidden coordinates, one fixed error vector is part of that
record for the entire execution. An adversary choosing a new error after
each query is a different information model. These objectives concern terminal
width under a hard budget, rather than expected requests until a winner
is certified. The expectations are well defined: the widths are Borel
measurable by Proposition~\ref{prop:risk_concavity_transcript}, and a
finite-valued monotone $\phi$ is Borel measurable and bounded on $[0,D_T]$.

For a required precision $\tau\geq0$, let $\mathcal C$ be either the
randomized batch or randomized adaptive policy class, and write
$Q_{\mathcal P}(y,\omega)$ for the terminal set under internal seed
$\omega$. Define its optimal worst-case precision-success probability by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 S_\tau^{\mathcal C}
 &=\sup_{\mathcal P\in\mathcal C}\inf_{y\in F}
   \Pr_\omega\!\left\{W_{Q_{\mathcal P}(y,\omega)}(y)\leq\tau\right\}\\
 &=1-R_{\mathcal C}^{\phi_\tau},\qquad
   \phi_\tau(w)=\boldsymbol{1}\{w>\tau\},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:risk_precision_success}
\end{equation}
where $R_{\mathcal C}^{\phi_\tau}$ denotes the corresponding risk in
\eqref{eq:risk_objectives}. The identity follows by complementing the
success event and reversing each supremum or infimum under subtraction
from one; it does not require an optimal policy to attain either bound.
The probability concerns the algorithm's seed at a fixed hidden record,
and equality at the threshold counts as success. Reaching this precision
does not require the interval to exclude zero, so it need not certify a
winning model.

\begin{proposition}[Concavity and the transcript fiber]
\label{prop:risk_concavity_transcript}
For every fixed $Q$, $W_Q$ is nonnegative, finite, concave, and upper
semicontinuous on $F$. For every adaptive execution, its terminal
information fiber is precisely $F_Q(y)$ for its terminal queried set $Q$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose maximizing and minimizing endpoints $u_k,v_k\in F_Q(y_k)$,
$k=1,2$. For $0\le\lambda\le1$, convexity of $F$ and linearity of the
measurements make $\lambda u_1+(1-\lambda)u_2$ and
$\lambda v_1+(1-\lambda)v_2$ feasible endpoints at
$\lambda y_1+(1-\lambda)y_2$. Their target difference is
$\lambda W_Q(y_1)+(1-\lambda)W_Q(y_2)$, proving concavity.
For upper semicontinuity, take $y_k\to y$ and a subsequence attaining
the limsup of the widths. Compactness supplies a further subsequence
whose endpoint pairs converge to $u,v\in F_Q(y)$. Its limiting
difference is at most $W_Q(y)$.

For the transcript claim, fix the internal random seed. If no query is
made, both fibers are $F$. Otherwise, any
$z\in F_Q(y)$ gives the same answer to the first queried measurement.
Inductively, equal previous answers produce the same next measurement
and the same next answer. The stopping decision is also unchanged.
Thus every completion agreeing with all queried answers follows the
same entire transcript. Conversely, any completion consistent with
that transcript must agree on every queried answer. Query choices and
the stopping time therefore impose no additional restrictions on the
fiber beyond those exact observations. This argument is pointwise in the
seed; no conditioning on a positive-probability seed event is required.
\end{proof}

\section{Which width penalties can benefit from adaptation?}
\label{sec:risk_classification}
The next result uses classical minimax theory. The contribution is the
resulting classification for sharp information-fiber widths and its
coordinate-query converse, rather than a new minimax or duality theorem.
The proof has two parts. For concave penalties, a single hardest record
limits every final query set, including those chosen adaptively; Jensen's
inequality supplies a batch policy meeting that limit. For any nonconcave
penalty, a three-coordinate construction realizes a strict midpoint
violation as an adaptive advantage. These arguments establish the universal
claim; the later numerical checks test implementations on particular inputs.

\begin{theorem}[Universal classification of width penalties]
\label{prop:risk_classification}
Let $\phi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be finite-valued and nondecreasing.
Randomized batch and randomized adaptive querying have equal minimax
$\phi$-risk for every problem satisfying Section~\ref{sec:risk_setup}
if and only if $\phi$ is concave. More precisely, put
\begin{equation}
 \rho=\min_{\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal Q)}\max_{y\in F}
         \sum_{Q\in\mathcal Q}\pi_Q W_Q(y).
 \label{eq:risk_rho}
\end{equation}
If $\phi$ is concave, then
\begin{equation}
 R_{\mathrm{adaptive}}^{\phi}
 =R_{\mathrm{batch}}^{\phi}=\phi(\rho).
 \label{eq:risk_concave_value}
\end{equation}
Every batch policy attaining~\eqref{eq:risk_rho} simultaneously attains
this equality for every such concave $\phi$. If $\phi$ is not concave,
a strict adaptive improvement already occurs on a three-coordinate
compact convex polytope, with all coordinates queryable at unit cost
and a hard budget of two.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The function $\sum_Q\pi_QW_Q(y)$ is affine and continuous in $\pi$,
and concave and upper semicontinuous in $y$. Sion's minimax theorem \citep{sion1958}
therefore yields
\begin{equation}
 \rho=\max_{y\in F}\min_{Q\in\mathcal Q}W_Q(y).
 \label{eq:risk_hardest_record}
\end{equation}
All values are finite. Writing $G(\pi)=\max_{y\in F}\sum_Q\pi_QW_Q(y)$,
the bound $|G(\pi)-G(\pi')|\le D_T\|\pi-\pi'\|_1$ proves continuity.
Thus the minimum in~\eqref{eq:risk_rho} is attained on the compact simplex.
The maximum in~\eqref{eq:risk_hardest_record} is attained because the
minimum of finitely many upper semicontinuous functions is upper
semicontinuous on compact $F$. Hence there is one record $y^\star\in F$
such that $W_Q(y^\star)\ge\rho$ for every admissible $Q$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:risk_concavity_transcript}, every execution of
every adaptive policy has terminal width at least $\rho$ at this same
record. Monotonicity gives the lower bound $\phi(\rho)$.

Let $\pi^\star$ attain~\eqref{eq:risk_rho}. Concavity, Jensen's
inequality, and monotonicity give, for every $y\in F$,
\[
 \sum_Q\pi^\star_Q\phi(W_Q(y))
 \le \phi\!\left(\sum_Q\pi^\star_QW_Q(y)\right)
 \le\phi(\rho).
\]
This proves the upper bound and simultaneous optimality. No continuity
of $\phi$ at zero is needed; the finite-mixture form of Jensen's
inequality suffices. For example, $\phi(w)=\boldsymbol{1}\{w>0\}$ is
concave on $[0,\infty)$ despite its jump at zero.

For necessity, a nonconcave, nondecreasing finite-valued $\phi$ must
have a strict midpoint-concavity violation: there exist $0\le l<h$ such
that, with $m=(l+h)/2$,
\begin{equation}
 \phi(m)<\{\phi(l)+\phi(h)\}/2.
 \label{eq:risk_midpoint_violation}
\end{equation}
Indeed, if midpoint concavity held, for $0\le x\le z$ and dyadic
$q_k\uparrow\theta$ its dyadic inequalities and monotonicity would give
\[
\phi((1-\theta)x+\theta z)
\ge\phi((1-q_k)x+q_kz)
\ge(1-q_k)\phi(x)+q_k\phi(z).
\]
Taking the limit only on the right proves full concavity without a
continuity assumption, a contradiction. Set $d_0=h-l$ and consider
\begin{equation}
 F_{l,h}=\{(a,b,c):0\le a\le1,\ 0\le b\le l+d_0a,
                  \ 0\le c\le h-d_0a\},\qquad T=ha+b+c.
 \label{eq:risk_converse_polytope}
\end{equation}
All three coordinates are available at unit cost, with budget two.
The widths after querying $\{a,b\}$ and $\{a,c\}$ are respectively
$h-d_0a$ and $l+d_0a$. An adaptive policy first observes $a$ and then
queries the coordinate with the larger remaining capacity. Its terminal
width is at most $m$, so its risk is at most $\phi(m)$.
At $(a,b,c)=(1/2,0,0)$, these two widths equal $m$, while the width
after querying $\{b,c\}$ is $h$. Thus every admissible terminal set
has width at least $m$, and the adaptive optimum is exactly $\phi(m)$.

Now put equal adversarial mass on $(0,0,0)$ and $(1,0,0)$.
Each batch $\{a,b\}$ or $\{a,c\}$ has average penalty
$\{\phi(l)+\phi(h)\}/2$. The batch $\{b,c\}$ has width $h$ at both
records, so its average penalty is no smaller. A batch using fewer
coordinates cannot improve upon an extension to two coordinates,
because more observations only shrink the fiber. Every randomized
batch policy consequently has worst-case risk at least
$\{\phi(l)+\phi(h)\}/2>\phi(m)$. This is the required strict separation.
\end{proof}

The universal quantifier is essential. A nonconcave penalty permits an
adaptive advantage somewhere in this class; it need not yield an
advantage for every feasible set. The theorem covers heterogeneous fixed
costs and overlapping constraints at the level of information theory.
It does not imply a polynomial-time optimizer for an arbitrary feasible
set or budget.

\section{S12. General dimensional precision bound}
\subsection{A precision consequence of sparse-policy bounds}
% Research draft: AI-assisted derivation and internal mathematical review.
% Not a human-specialist validation or historical-priority claim.
The dimension bound below adapts established sparse-policy reasoning.
\citet[Theorem~2 and Example~5]{kurtz2026} gives the corresponding
dimension-plus-one policy bound for continuous concave scenario costs,
including conditional information discovery. Conditional terminal widths
fit that framework on polytopes. The direct Helly proof below also allows
compact convex $F$, for which these widths are upper semicontinuous but
need not be continuous, and translates the sparse cover into precision
success under uniform randomization.
\begin{theorem}[A dimension-dependent batch guarantee, adapted from sparse-policy bounds]
\label{thm:helly-precision}
Let $F$ be nonempty, compact and convex, and let $T(x)=c^\top x+d$.
Fix a first-stage exact linear observation $L_0x$, whose feasible-answer
set $I=L_0F$ has affine dimension $r$. After this answer, a deterministic
policy chooses one of finitely many second-stage linear measurement
batches $L_1,\ldots,L_m$. Every combined batch $(L_0,L_j)$ must obey the
same declared hard budget, and the eligible second-stage batches must not
depend on the first answer. Suppose the policy's terminal sharp interval
has width at most $\tau$ for every $x\in F$.
Then some randomized nonadaptive policy supported on at most $r+1$
of these combined batches has worst-case precision success at least
$1/(r+1)$.
In particular, a sure-precision policy using two scalar queries yields
a randomized-batch success guarantee of at least $1/2$, even when its
conditional worst widths are not affine functions of the first answer.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $t\in I$, define the conditional worst terminal width
\[
 g_j(t)=\max\{c^\top(u-v):u,v\in F,\ L_0u=L_0v=t,
                                      \ L_ju=L_jv\}.
\]
The feasible set is nonempty and compact, so the maximum is attained.
The expression is exactly the largest terminal sharp width among records
with first answer $t$: an endpoint pair with equal first and second
answers belongs to a common terminal fiber, and the endpoints of every
such fiber are feasible in this maximization.

Each $g_j$ is concave on $I$. Indeed, convex combinations of optimizing
endpoint pairs at $t_1,t_2$ are feasible at the corresponding convex
combination of answers, and attain the same convex combination of their
objective values. The function is also upper semicontinuous: for a
convergent sequence of answers, compactness gives convergent subsequences
of optimizing endpoints, whose limits are feasible at the limiting
answer. Consequently, $g_j$ is finite and measurable.

For a fixed first answer, the policy chooses a single second-stage batch;
its sure guarantee therefore implies $\min_jg_j(t)\leq\tau$ throughout
$I$. The strict failure sets
\[
 H_j=\{t\in I:g_j(t)>\tau\}
\]
are convex by concavity, and $\bigcap_{j=1}^m H_j=\varnothing$.
Finite Helly's theorem \citep[Theorem~1.1]{amenta2016}, applied in the affine hull of $I$, gives a
subfamily $J$ of at most $r+1$ sets with empty intersection. This finite
form of Helly's theorem does not require the sets to be closed; thus
using strict failure sets preserves success at equality with $\tau$.

Choose uniformly among the combined batches indexed by $J$. For every
fixed record $x$, its answer $t=L_0x$ lies outside at least one $H_j$,
so that batch's actual terminal width is at most $g_j(t)\leq\tau$.
Its precision success probability is therefore at least $1/|J|$, and
hence at least $1/(r+1)$, for every record.
\end{proof}


The empty second-stage action includes stopping after the first stage.
Fixed unequal costs are allowed if all combined batches obey the budget;
the action dictionary and latent record are fixed before randomization.
The $K$-capacity construction in Online Resource~1, S9 is sharp
with first-answer dimension $r=K-1$ and optimal batch success $1/K$.
For scalar first answers, at most two second-stage actions suffice to
retain the optimal sure-width guarantee. On polytopes, test each singleton
or pair of actions with the shared-first-answer LP in
Online Resource~1, S10; there are at most
$\binom m2+m$ candidates. This sparse policy need not minimize conditional
width at every individual answer. Helly-based sparsification for
convex-function optimization is classical
\citep[Section~2, Theorem~1]{rockafellar1965}. The contribution here is
its certificate-risk interpretation and sharpness through admissible
information queries, rather than a new general sparse-policy theorem.
\section{S13. Scalar cumulative-counter planning and feedback triage}
\subsection{Adaptive precision from cumulative-counter queries}
\label{sec:counter-precision-method}
Consider a six-hour block with nonnegative hourly increments
$z=(z_1,\ldots,z_6)$ and retained cumulative registers $C_0,C_6$,
all expressed in the same energy units.
Writing $E=C_6-C_0$, the initial feasible set is
$F_E=\{z\geq0:\sum_{h=1}^6z_h=E\}$.
For two frozen forecast vectors $a,b$, their block MSE contrast is
\[
 D(z)=d+c^\top z,\qquad
 d=\frac16\sum_{h=1}^6(a_h^2-b_h^2),\quad
 c_h=\frac{2(b_h-a_h)}6.
\]
The permitted query at boundary $k\in\{1,\ldots,5\}$ returns
$C_k$, equivalently the prefix sum $s_k=C_k-C_0$.
The budget is at most two distinct internal registers per pair and block.
This interface retrieves cumulative registers. The coordinate-increment
recovery controls are documented separately in Online Resource~1, S2--S4.
The register queries are exact linear measurements covered by the general
information model. Adaptive scalar certification also appears in monotone
integral estimation \citep[Section~4.1]{gaillard2023}; here the target is an
affine contrast of interval increments under a fixed hard budget.

For integer boundaries $0\leq p<q\leq6$, define
$r(p,q)=\max_{p<h\leq q}c_h-\min_{p<h\leq q}c_h$.
With queried boundaries $Q$, let $0=q_0<\cdots<q_m=6$ include
the outer boundaries and $Q$, and put $s_0=0$, $s_6=E$.
Nonnegativity and the segment totals give the sharp width
\begin{equation}
 W_Q(z)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}
 (s_{q_\ell}-s_{q_{\ell-1}})r(q_{\ell-1},q_\ell).
 \label{eq:counter-width}
\end{equation}
Both endpoints are attainable by placing each segment's mass at a
minimum- or maximum-coefficient hour. The initial width is $W_0=Er(0,6)$.
Tasks with $W_0=0$ are already precise and require no queries.

\begin{proposition}[Two best-side splits for an arbitrary block]\label{prop:counter-two-query}
Let $n\geq3$, $E>0$, $x\geq0$, $\sum_{h=1}^n x_h=E$, and let the target be
$d+\sum_hc_hx_h$. Each query returns one internal prefix sum. Write
$r(p,q)=\max_{p<h\leq q}c_h-\min_{p<h\leq q}c_h$.
For first boundary $k$, put $\ell_k=r(0,k)$ and $r_k=r(k,n)$.
Define each of the following quantities whenever its corresponding
side has an eligible second boundary:
\[
 \alpha_k=\min_{1\leq j<k}\max\{r(0,j),r(j,k)\},\qquad
 \beta_k=\min_{k<j<n}\max\{r(k,j),r(j,n)\}.
\]
Choose a minimizing boundary on each side, breaking ties by smallest index.
When both sides are nonempty, for normalized first answer $t=s_k/E$,
the optimal conditional worst width is
\[
 E\min\{\alpha_k t+r_k(1-t),\ \ell_k t+\beta_k(1-t)\}.
\]
Thus only one best boundary on each side is needed, independently of $t$.
If $\Delta_k=\ell_k+r_k-\alpha_k-\beta_k>0$, the switch is
\[
 t_k^\star=\frac{r_k-\beta_k}{\Delta_k}\in[0,1],
\]
and the worst width for this first boundary is
\[
 A_k=E\max\left\{\alpha_k,\beta_k,
          \frac{\ell_kr_k-\alpha_k\beta_k}{\Delta_k}\right\}.
\]
Use the best right split below the switch and the best left split above
it; either is valid at equality. If $\Delta_k=0$, the two lines coincide
and $A_k=E\max\{\ell_k,r_k\}$. At $k=1$ only the right split exists and
$A_1=E\beta_1$; at $k=n-1$ only the left split exists and
$A_{n-1}=E\alpha_{n-1}$. The optimal deterministic two-query guarantee is
\begin{equation} A=\min_{1\leq k<n} A_k.\label{eq:counter-adaptive-value}\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
All second queries left of $k$ have the common right-side contribution
$r_k(1-t)$; minimizing their remaining term $t$ times a split range
selects the same minimizing boundary for every $t\geq0$. The right-side
argument is identical. Moreover $\alpha_k\leq\ell_k$ and
$\beta_k\leq r_k$. Hence $\Delta_k\geq0$, and when it is positive the
displayed intersection belongs to $[0,1]$. The maximum of the lower
envelope occurs at an endpoint or that intersection. Its endpoint
values are $\beta_k,\alpha_k$, and its intersection value is the displayed
quotient. If $\Delta_k=0$, both nonnegative differences vanish, so the
lines coincide. At an outer eligible boundary the single-hour side has
zero coefficient range, giving the stated one-line values.
\end{proof}

All contiguous ranges can be built in $O(n^2)$ arithmetic operations
and stored in $O(n^2)$ space. Scanning the candidates for every
$\alpha_k,\beta_k$ costs another $O(n^2)$ operations; the closed forms
then cost $O(n)$. This is an arithmetic-operation bound for the exact-total,
uncapped two-prefix problem, not a bit-complexity or noisy-polytope claim.
For $n=2$, the single internal register determines both increments;
for $n=1$ the total already determines the only increment.


\paragraph{An upper certificate for randomized-batch precision.}
Returning to the six-hour case, there are ten two-boundary batches.
Let $z^{(h)}=Ee_h$ be the six feasible
single-hour mass profiles, and define
$M_{hQ}(\tau)=\mathbf1\{W_Q(z^{(h)})\leq\tau\}$.
The finite-profile program
\begin{equation}
 U(\tau)=\min_{w\geq0,\,\sum_hw_h=1,\,u}u
 \quad\text{subject to}\quad
 \sum_hw_hM_{hQ}(\tau)\leq u\quad\text{for every batch }Q
 \label{eq:counter-precision-upper}
\end{equation}
upper-bounds the worst-case precision success of \emph{every} randomized
batch policy. Indeed, its minimum success over $F_E$ is at most its success
averaged over these profiles under $w$, which is at most
$\max_Q\sum_hw_hM_{hQ}$. This remains valid for batches with fewer queries:
adding an unused register cannot increase width. The exported $w$ therefore
provides a checkable upper certificate. The finite profile set need not
contain a worst completion for an indicator objective, so $U(\tau)$ is not
asserted to equal the full precision optimum in general. Below, write
$S_B^\star=S_\tau^{\mathrm{batch}}$ and
$S_A=S_\tau^{\mathrm{adaptive}}$ for the precision-success values defined
in \eqref{eq:risk_precision_success}, at this fixed instance and threshold.
If $A\leq\tau$ and $U(\tau)<1$, the adaptive policy succeeds for every
completion, whereas no randomized batch can do so. In floating-point
classification, batch widths within $10^{-9}W_0$ above the threshold count
as success, and adaptive certification requires a margin of
$10^{-9}W_0$; exact constructed boundary cases are treated analytically.

\paragraph{An exact counter example.}
Take $E=1$ and $c=(0,0,1,1,0,1)$, so $W_0=1$.
Query boundary $4$ first, obtaining $t=s_4$.
Batch $\{2,4\}$ has width $1-t$, while batch $\{4,5\}$ has width $t$.
Querying $5$ when $t<1/2$ and $2$ otherwise thus guarantees
width $\min\{t,1-t\}\leq1/2$.
Conversely, at $z=e_5$ only batch $\{4,5\}$ attains width below $1$;
that batch has width $1$ at $z=e_2$. No batch succeeds at both profiles
for any threshold $\tau<1$. Weights $w_2=w_5=1/2$ in
\eqref{eq:counter-precision-upper} give $U\leq1/2$.
An equal mixture of $\{2,4\}$ and $\{4,5\}$ succeeds with probability
at least $1/2$ on every completion when $\tau\geq1/2$.
Consequently, for every $\tau\in[1/2,1)$ the optimal adaptive worst-case
success is $1$ and the optimal randomized-batch worst-case success is
$1/2$.

\begin{corollary}[The two-register half-success floor]
\label{prop:counter-half-floor}
If a deterministic two-register policy guarantees width at most $\tau$,
a randomized batch supported on at most two batches has worst-case
precision success at least $1/2$. Thus an adverse-profile upper bound
$U(\tau)=1/2$ proves the exact batch optimum for such an instance.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:helly-precision} with scalar first answer.
For the exact-total prefix model the conditional functions are affine:
their good sets are prefixes or suffixes of $[0,1]$. The longest good
prefix and earliest good suffix cover all answers; mix their batches
equally, breaking geometric ties by smallest second-query index.
\end{proof}
% Independently reviewed operational corollary; no new historical-priority claim.
\begin{corollary}[Deciding whether feedback is necessary]
\label{cor:counter-feedback-triage}
In the cumulative-counter model, let $\mathcal B$ contain the ten
two-register batches, and define
\[
 D=\min_{Q\in\mathcal B}\max_{z\in F_E}W_Q(z)
   =\min_{Q\in\mathcal B}\max_{1\leq h\leq6}W_Q(Ee_h).
\]
Let $A$ be the exact deterministic adaptive value in
\eqref{eq:counter-adaptive-value}, and let $U(\tau)$ be the
finite-profile upper certificate in \eqref{eq:counter-precision-upper}.
For every $\tau\geq0$,
\[
 D\leq\tau
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad U(\tau)=1
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S_B^\star=1.
\]
Consequently, the available forecasts and outer registers determine
three mutually exclusive decisions before any optional retrieval:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $D\leq\tau$, a fixed batch guarantees the requested precision.
\item If $A\leq\tau<D$, feedback enables a deterministic guarantee that
      every randomized batch fails to match.
\item If $\tau<A$, no deterministic adaptive policy with two register
      queries guarantees the requested precision.
\end{enumerate}
In the second case,
$1/2\leq S_B^\star\leq U(\tau)\leq5/6$.
The lower bound uses Corollary~\ref{prop:counter-half-floor}; when
$U(\tau)=1/2$, the randomized-batch optimum is exactly $1/2$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For a fixed batch, \eqref{eq:counter-width} is a linear function of
$z$. It therefore attains its maximum over the energy simplex at a
concentration profile $Ee_h$, which proves the expression for $D$.
If $D\leq\tau$, one batch succeeds at all six profiles and, by
linearity, at every completion. Thus $S_B^\star=1$ and $U(\tau)=1$.
If $D>\tau$, every batch fails at at least one profile. The uniform
distribution on the six profiles consequently assigns every batch
success probability at most $5/6$. It is a feasible adverse distribution
in \eqref{eq:counter-precision-upper}, so
$S_B^\star\leq U(\tau)\leq5/6<1$. This proves both equivalences.
Adaptation includes fixed batches, hence $A\leq D$. The three cases
then follow from the defining optimality of $A$ and $D$.
\end{proof}

This decision rule uses only the forecast coefficients, retained total,
query dictionary and requested precision. It identifies when a sequential
query response is necessary for a guarantee, without measuring retrieval
latency, financial cost or the frequency of adverse energy profiles.
The finite-profile program is decisive for whether batch success equals
one; it remains only an upper bound on intermediate success values unless
a matching guarantee is supplied.
\section{S14. A separate mean joint-width separation}
\section{Exact shared-query separations: policies, lower bounds, and verifiable certificates}
\label{sec:joint-separations}
An auditor comparing several models can reuse each reading across their
loss contrasts. Let $W_{r,Q}(y)$ be the sharp width for contrast $r$ after
the common transcript $Q$, and let $W_{r,0}>0$ be its initial width. Define
\[
 J_Q(y)=\max_r W_{r,Q}(y)/W_{r,0}.
\]
Then $J_Q(y)\leq1/2$ means that every requested comparison has reached
half its original width. Already determined contrasts can be omitted.
This is the local diameter of the vector contrast map in a weighted
$\ell_\infty$ norm, within the established vector-recovery framework
\citep{krieg2025}; shared-label model certification also has close prior
work \citep{kuboyama2026}.

\begin{theorem}[Feedback reduces mean joint width with two shared queries]
\label{thm:shared-prefix-separation}
Let $F=\{x\in\mathbb R_+^6:\sum_hx_h=1\}$, and allow at most two
queries from $P_kx=\sum_{h\leq k}x_h$, $k=1,\ldots,5$.  For the two
contrasts with coefficient vectors
\[
 c^{(1)}=(1/2,0,1,1/2,1/2,0),\qquad
 c^{(2)}=(2/3,2/3,1,1/3,0,1),
\]
both initial widths are one.  Let $J_Q(x)$ be the larger terminal sharp
width and let $R^J_{\mathcal C}=\inf_{P\in\mathcal C}\sup_{x\in F}
\mathbb E J_{Q_P(x)}(x)$.  Then
\[
 R^J_{\mathrm{randomized\ adaptive}}=\tfrac12
 <\tfrac23=R^J_{\mathrm{randomized\ batch}}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A batch partitions the six coordinates into segments.  Its scalar width
is the sum, over segments, of segment mass times coefficient range.
After querying boundary~3 with answer $t$, the worst remaining joint
widths for second queries~5 and~2 are, respectively,
\[
 G_{35}(t)=\max(t,1/3),\qquad
 G_{32}(t)=\max(1/2,1-t).
\]
Query~5 when $t\leq1/2$ and query~2 otherwise.  This guarantees $J\leq1/2$.
At the single feasible record $x^*=(0,0,1/2,0,0,1/2)$, all ten final
two-query sets have $J_Q(x^*)\geq1/2$; their values are the first row below.
Here and below $ij$ means boundaries $\{i,j\}$.
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccccccccc}
Q&12&13&14&15&23&24&25&34&35&45\\\hline
J_Q(x^*)&1&3/4&5/6&1/2&1/2&5/6&1/2&3/4&1/2&1/2\\
\mathbb E_\mu J_Q&2/3&2/3&2/3&2/3&2/3&13/18&5/6&5/6&2/3&2/3
\end{array}
\]
For a fixed internal random seed, every completion agreeing with the
queried answers follows the same adaptive transcript and stopping rule.
Its terminal interval is therefore the final-set interval used above.
Fewer queries cannot decrease width.  Thus every randomized adaptive
policy has expected width at least $1/2$ at $x^*$.

For the batch upper bound, mix $12,23,35$ with probabilities
$1/6,1/3,1/2$.  Its expected joint widths at $e_1,\ldots,e_6$ are
$(2/3,2/3,2/3,2/3,2/3,1/2)$.  Each $J_Q$ is a maximum of linear segment
widths and hence convex; these vertex values bound the expectation
throughout $F$ by $2/3$.  For the reverse bound, place adverse masses
$\mu=(1/3,0,1/6,1/6,1/3,0)$ on those vertices.  The table's second row
shows that every batch has expectation at least $2/3$, and therefore so
does every batch mixture.  Its worst-record expectation is no smaller.
\end{proof}
This is a constructed separation for \emph{mean joint width}, not a
precision-success probability.  It does not follow from the scalar
classification: a maximum of concave local widths need not be concave.
The argument uses established vector recovery and finite-game bounds
\citep{krieg2025}; its claim is the explicit shared-prefix separation.
The two contrasts can be realized with a common forecast baseline
$a=0$ and $b^{(r)}=3c^{(r)}$ in a six-target paired MSE.  Adding other
contrasts would define a different joint objective.
For the uncapped exact-total simplex, each batch's scalar widths are
linear in the energy allocation. Thus $J_Q$ is convex. Its worst mixture
expectation is attained at a concentration vertex, making the finite-game
LP exact for randomized-batch mean risk. The deterministic two-query
planner instead minimizes the worst joint width after feedback. Its
conditional objective is the maximum of the contrast-specific affine
widths; checking all line intersections gives the exact optimum.
The two objectives remain distinct. A deterministic adaptive value below
the randomized-batch lower bound establishes an adaptive advantage; the
reverse inequality does not establish optimality among adaptive policies.
The vertex argument does not transfer to precision indicators, caps or
measurement error. S8 provides the formulations and verification details.

with the analytic constructions, with largest width discrepancy
$3.34\times10^{-16}$ against a $2\times10^{-8}$ scale-aware tolerance.
Six explicit polytopes check overlapping constraints, signed contrasts,
boxes and coupled capacities. All 37 enumerated batches and 24 possible
first queries agree with the corresponding LP guarantees. These are small
formulation checks, not a general scalability claim.

For coordinate queries under disjoint exact totals and unit costs, a
supporting compact mean-width solver avoids enumerating batches.
Its retained-endpoint flow representation and feasible dual completion
and adaptive lower-bound proof are given in Online Resource~1, S5;
S7 supplies the measured comparisons. General weighted-oracle
and dynamic-program LP constructions are established
\citep{kawase2024,martin1990}. The specialized representation and its
hard-budget policy decomposition are the implementation contribution.

All 28 archived compact settings solved. Twelve prescribed enumerated
comparisons agreed within $1.27\times10^{-15}$ after normalization.
Compact was faster in eight and used less whole-process peak memory in
six; enumeration was faster in the four smallest one-block settings.
The largest tested LP had 38,820 variables and ran in 9.933--10.012 seconds
for 48 target coordinates. These are single-run measurements on a shared
Intel Core i7-8565U host with one numerical thread. The complete timing,
memory, residual, tie-profile and skip ledgers are in S1/S7.
No large-scale optimizer for arbitrary convex sets or precision risk is implied.
\subsection{Historical controls: guarantees and observed accuracy}
\label{sec:historical-application}
The completed-archive control \citep{portugal,uci} incorporates every permitted forecast
copy before evaluation. Under identical information and zero new readings,
paired bounds certify 7,836/9,720 Portugal comparisons (80.62\%), versus
2,513 (25.85\%) from separate-loss bounds. However, a donor imputer makes
no observed whole-period errors at full coverage; the added value is an
all-completions certificate, not demonstrated prediction accuracy.
In the daily archive analysis, free reconstruction already resolves 95.33\%
of masked targets, exact acquisition adds only 0.042 percentage points over
greedy, and robust randomization worsens realized width despite improving
its objective. Outcome-independent optimal-face refinements retain that
tradeoff \citep{iancu2014}. The coordinate-acquisition control reports a
0.9738064\% mean acquisition saving. S2--S4 give its denominator, these adverse controls,
all 324 grid cells, four-model gates, bootstrap details and full protocols.
They explain why the present paper evaluates the assurance requirement and
retrieval interface explicitly rather than claiming general acquisition savings.
\subsection{Full implementation and source-to-table checks}
\paragraph{Solver and information-flow checks.}
All policies reuse the same two counter answers across the six contrasts.
Plans are saved before recorded answers are loaded; this verifies the new
run's dependency order, not prospective independence of the reused data.
A separate implementation reconstructs every plan and summary on all 1,098
blocks. On 18 fixed representative blocks it solves 19,440 direct endpoint
LPs and 6,480 conditional LPs. Maximum discrepancies are
$2.22\times10^{-16}$ and $1.11\times10^{-16}$; the numerical check tolerance
is $10^{-8}$. The largest batch primal--dual gap is $2.03\times10^{-10}$.
The numerical adaptive worst-width value is below the optimal randomized
batch mean value on 462 original and 162 external blocks. This is a
subset result, not uniform dominance; the full-panel mean differences are
only 0.00177 and 0.00393 of initial width.

An outcome-informed diagnostic optimizes recorded outcomes over the
mean-optimal batch face, allowing $10^{-9}$ objective slack. Even its best
precision success is 54.285512\% original and 69.898131\% external,
essentially the declared mixtures' 54.285508\% and 69.898130\%.
Thus arbitrary LP tie selection does not explain this comparison. These
diagnostic oracle choices are not deployable acquisition policies, nor
optimal batches for worst-case precision success. S8 supplies all ledgers,
per-meter results, exact bounded-error certificates and reproduction commands.
